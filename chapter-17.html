A bent strip relaxes after its load changes. That response can arise from local interstitial reorientation, from solute moving across the whole thickness, or from mechanisms unrelated to diffusion. A relaxation time becomes a diffusion coefficient only after geometry, coupling, and the measured loss convention have been specified.

Lecture 38 supplies mechanical and electrical methods; Lecture 40 supplies relaxation, Haven-ratio, carrier-profile, Snoek, and Gorsky problems. The Snoek and Gorsky numerical inputs were visually checked at 16:47–19:49. The standard-linear-solid and RC plots use separately declared synthetic parameters.

17.1 Delayed reversible strain produces a frequency-dependent loss L38 00:22

Elastic strain responds reversibly and effectively instantaneously on the observation time scale. Anelastic strain is reversible but delayed: an internal population or configuration approaches the state favored by applied stress. Plastic deformation instead leaves a permanent strain in the elementary distinction used here. Real specimens can combine these responses, so a measured phase lag need not have a single microscopic origin.

Let stress \(\sigma\) and strain \(\varepsilon\) vary sinusoidally at angular frequency \(\omega\), in rad/s. Represent the linear response with complex Young's modulus \(E^*=E'+iE''\) under an \(e^{i\omega t}\) convention. The storage modulus \(E'\) and positive loss modulus \(E''\) are in pascals. For imposed strain amplitude \(\varepsilon_0\), the dissipated energy per volume in one cycle is \(\Delta W=\pi E''\varepsilon_0^2\); the storage-energy scale is \(W=E'\varepsilon_0^2/2\). The conventional inverse quality factor is consequently \(Q^{-1}=\Delta W/(2\pi W)=E''/E'=\tan\delta\), with loss angle delta. Different energy normalizations must be identified rather than hidden in a factor of pi.

For the real imposed strain \(\varepsilon(t)=\varepsilon_0\cos\omega t\), the stress is \(\sigma(t)=\varepsilon_0(E'\cos\omega t-E''\sin\omega t)\). Therefore the work per cycle is \(\Delta W=\int_0^{2\pi/\omega}\sigma\dot\varepsilon\,dt=\pi E''\varepsilon_0^2\): the mixed sine–cosine term integrates to zero and the squared-sine term supplies the positive dissipation.

To construct a single-time constitutive model, introduce a dimensionless internal strain coordinate \(h(t)\) that approaches the imposed strain according to \(\tau_\sigma\dot h+h=\varepsilon\), where \(\tau_\sigma\gt0\) is in seconds. Let \(\sigma=E_\infty\varepsilon-(E_\infty-E_0)h\), with relaxed modulus \(E_0\gt0\) and unrelaxed modulus \(E_\infty\gt E_0\), both in pascals. Immediately after a strain step from equilibrium, h has not changed and the modulus is \(E_\infty\); after h reaches the imposed strain, the modulus is \(E_0\).

For harmonic amplitudes, the internal equation becomes \((1+i\omega\tau_\sigma)\hat h=\hat\varepsilon\). Substituting into the stress equation gives the standard-linear-solid response \(E^*=\hat\sigma/\hat\varepsilon=E_\infty-(E_\infty-E_0)/(1+i\omega\tau_\sigma)\). Eliminating h also gives \(\sigma+\tau_\sigma\dot\sigma=E_0\varepsilon+E_\infty\tau_\sigma\dot\varepsilon\). At fixed strain the stress relaxes with time \(\tau_\sigma\); at fixed stress the strain relaxes with creep time \(\tau_\varepsilon=\tau_\sigma E_\infty/E_0\). Rationalizing the complex modulus gives

\[ E'=E_\infty-\frac{E_\infty-E_0}{1+y^2},\qquad E''=\frac{(E_\infty-E_0)y}{1+y^2},\quad y=\omega\tau_\sigma. \tag{17.1} \]

The normalized Debye modulus loss peaks at \(y=1\), since differentiating \(y/(1+y^2)\) gives \((1-y^2)/(1+y^2)^2\). But the loss tangent is \((E_\infty-E_0)y/(E_0+E_\infty y^2)\), whose peak is at \(y=\sqrt{E_0/E_\infty}\). Defining \(\tau_R=\tau_\sigma\sqrt{E_\infty/E_0}=\sqrt{\tau_\sigma\tau_\varepsilon}\), with creep time \(\tau_\varepsilon=\tau_\sigma E_\infty/E_0\), places that peak at \(\omega\tau_R=1\). The geometric mean is not an arithmetic mean.

The distinction matters in a temperature sweep. If the relevant relaxation time obeys \(\tau=\tau_0\exp[H/(RT)]\), with activation enthalpy \(H>0\) in J/mol, a fixed-convention peak gives \(\ln\omega=-\ln\tau_0-H/(RT_p)\). The slope against inverse peak temperature is \(-H/R\). Relaxation time decreases as temperature increases; the positive sign in its Arrhenius exponent is consistent with the inverse rate. Temperature-dependent strengths or broad distributions can shift and broaden the peak beyond this single-time model.

17.2 Snoek relaxation measures local orientation changes L38 11:40

An interstitial atom can distort its local lattice anisotropically. A stress then favors some orientations of that distortion over others. Redistribution among equivalent orientations produces delayed strain even if an atom moves only over a few site spacings. The Snoek effect in a dilute BCC interstitial model arises from this stress-driven reorientation among octahedral-site classes. A relaxation peak reports an orientation-population time; connecting it to diffusion requires the jump geometry and rate convention.

For the simplest ideal BCC octahedral network, each site has four nearest allowed neighbors at distance \(a/2\), where \(a\) is the cubic lattice parameter. Let \(w\), in s⁻¹, be the rate to each individual neighbor. The total jump rate is \(\Gamma=4w\). With unbiased uncorrelated jump vectors, the three-dimensional random-walk relation gives \(D=\Gamma(a/2)^2/6=wa^2/6\).

The three orientation classes each connect to two neighbors in either other class. If \(p_x,p_y,p_z\) are their probabilities, the unforced rate equation is \(\dot p_x=2w(p_y+p_z-2p_x)\), and similarly for the others; their sum remains one. Write \(p_\alpha=1/3+\delta p_\alpha\), where \(\alpha=x,y,z\) and \(\delta p_x+\delta p_y+\delta p_z=0\). The anisotropic deviations then obey \(\dot{\delta p}_x=2w(\delta p_y+\delta p_z-2\delta p_x)=-6w\delta p_x\), with the same rate for the other components. Their population relaxation time is \(\tau_S=1/(6w)\). Combining this with the jump diffusivity gives

\[ D=\frac{a^2}{36\tau_S}. \tag{17.2} \]

This factor 36 encodes the specific octahedral geometry and rate definitions. Interactions, trapping, blocked sites, or another jump network can change it. Strong coupling between stress and strain can also distinguish an inferred mechanical loss time from the bare orientation-population time; the dilute weak-relaxation approximation must be stated when equating them.

The visually checked tutorial gives \(a=0.287\) nm and relaxation time one microsecond. In the model above, conversion to meters and seconds gives \(D=2.28803\times10^{-15}\) m²/s, consistent with the displayed rounded answer \(2.3\times10^{-15}\) m²/s. The original statement says only a cubic metal, but the supplied Snoek relation presumes this BCC interstitial model. Cubic symmetry by itself does not specify the jump network.

17.3 Gorsky relaxation requires transport across a specimen L38 18:52

Bending a strip produces a stress gradient across its thickness. If a solute changes the local strain energy, the gradient can drive it toward the more favorable side. This Gorsky effect involves long-range redistribution and therefore depends on specimen dimensions, unlike a purely local orientation process. A time scale that changes with thickness is a useful discriminator, provided geometry and boundary conditions are controlled.

After subtracting the stress-biased equilibrium profile, let a small concentration perturbation \(u(x,t)\) satisfy \(u_t=D_{\rm chem}u_{xx}\) on a strip of thickness \(d\), with zero normal flux at both faces. The slowest nonconstant mode is \(u\propto\cos(\pi x/d)e^{-\pi^2D_{\rm chem}t/d^2}\). Hence

\[ \tau_G=\frac{d^2}{\pi^2D_{\rm chem}},\qquad D_{\rm chem}\simeq\Phi D^* \tag{17.3} \]

The second relation is an additional dilute diagonal-mobility approximation with thermodynamic factor Phi. The mode derivation supplies the thickness-squared dependence; it does not supply the tracer coefficient or Phi from the relaxation data separately.

The source tutorial compares a 0.5 mm strip at 300 °C with a 1 mm strip at 400 °C, supplies Arrhenius constants \(D_0=2.3\times10^{-4}\) m²/s and \(Q=280\) kJ/mol, and asks to neglect thermodynamic-factor variation. Its wording calls those constants host-metal self-diffusion data even though the response concerns solute B. A quantitative B relaxation prediction needs the B mobility; the calculation below is conditional on using the supplied constants as its proxy. Host self diffusion does not establish that proxy physically.

Using absolute temperatures 573.15 and 673.15 K gives proxy coefficients \(6.98435\times10^{-30}\) and \(4.31421\times10^{-26}\) m²/s. With common Phi, \(\tau_1/\tau_2=(d_1/d_2)^2D_2/D_1=1544.24\). The thinner but colder specimen relaxes much more slowly in this model. Setting Phi to one gives enormous times \(3.62672\times10^{21}\) and \(2.34855\times10^{18}\) s. That scale is another reason to treat the inputs as an exercise rather than an experimentally practical relaxation protocol. The ratio direction is stated explicitly because reversing it gives its reciprocal.

17.4 Electrical measurements see charge transport and active carriers L38 22:59

For one mobile ionic species with number density \(n_c\) in m⁻³ and charge \(q_e\) in coulombs, a simple independent-carrier Einstein model gives conductivity \(\sigma=n_cq_e^2D_\sigma/(k_BT)\), in S/m. Here \(k_B\) is the Boltzmann constant and \(D_\sigma\) is the conductivity diffusivity defined by that relation. Conductivity weights collective charge displacement, whereas tracer diffusion weights individual tagged-particle displacement. Correlations can make them differ.

Using the chapter's convention, the Haven ratio is \(H_R=D^*/D_\sigma\). Its interpretation requires a consistent carrier density and the same species, phase, and conditions. Electronic conduction, grain-boundary resistance, electrode polarization, and multiple mobile ionic species can contaminate a naive single-carrier inversion. Measuring conductivity alone does not uniquely identify both carrier concentration and diffusivity.

Impedance spectroscopy separates responses by frequency. A synthetic parallel resistor–capacitor element has \(Z(\omega)=R_e/(1+i\omega R_eC_e)\), with resistance \(R_e\) in ohms and capacitance \(C_e\) in farads. Writing real and imaginary parts gives a semicircle \((\Re Z-R_e/2)^2+(-\Im Z)^2=(R_e/2)^2\). The plotted sign convention uses negative imaginary impedance. Its peak is at \(\omega R_eC_e=1\).

Figure 17.1 uses \(R_e=1000\) ohms and \(C_e=10^{-6}\) F, giving peak angular frequency 1000 rad/s and peak negative imaginary impedance 500 ohms. A series resistance would shift the circle; additional phases or interfaces can add arcs. Identifying an equivalent-circuit element with a specific physical process requires more evidence than fitting a semicircle.

Spreading resistance profiling measures a local electrical resistance on a prepared semiconductor section or bevel. Conversion to carrier concentration needs geometry and mobility calibration. Electrically active dopants change carrier populations, so this method can track diffusion-related activation profiles in materials such as silicon or gallium arsenide. It does not directly count every chemical dopant atom: inactive complexes, compensation, and mobility changes can separate chemical concentration from electrically active concentration.

The laboratory evaluates the verified tutorial inputs, labels the host-to-solute proxy assumption, checks the loss-tangent peak convention, and draws the RC semicircle. Saved output reports the enormous conditional Gorsky times rather than silently presenting them as a practical measurement. It fits no experimental loss spectrum or impedance data.

17.5 Exercises with complete solutions

1. Which loss peaks at one?

For \(E_0=100\) and \(E_\infty=120\) in common modulus units, compare the peak positions of normalized modulus loss and loss tangent.

Worked solution

The normalized modulus loss is \(y/(1+y^2)\), so its derivative vanishes at y one. The loss tangent is \(20y/(100+120y^2)\). Differentiating gives a numerator proportional to \(100-120y^2\), so its peak is \(y=\sqrt{100/120}=0.912871\). Rescaling time to the geometric mean relaxation time puts that peak at \(\omega\tau_R=1\). The phrase “Debye peak” must therefore identify the response and time convention.

2. Recover the Snoek factor

Use four jumps per site, individual rate w, jump length a/2, and orientation decay rate 6w to derive (17.2).

Worked solution

The total rate is 4w. The uncorrelated three-dimensional coefficient is \(D=4w(a/2)^2/6=wa^2/6\). The population time is \(\tau_S=1/(6w)\), so \(w=1/(6\tau_S)\) and \(D=a^2/(36\tau_S)\). Inserting 0.287 nm and one microsecond gives \(2.28803\times10^{-15}\) m²/s. Using total rate in place of individual w in the orientation equation would create a coordination-factor error.

3. Interpret an activation plot

Derive the slope of ln angular frequency versus inverse peak temperature and explain why a positive activation enthalpy makes relaxation faster at high temperature.

Worked solution

At the specified single-time peak, \(\omega\tau_0e^{H/(RT_p)}=1\). Taking logarithms gives slope \(-H/R\) against \(1/T_p\). As temperature rises, \(H/(RT)\) decreases, so the positive exponential in the time decreases. Rates have the opposite Arrhenius sign because they are proportional to inverse time. Changes of relaxation strength or multiple times require a more detailed peak model.

4. State the Gorsky ratio direction

For the two verified tutorial strips, calculate sample-one time divided by sample-two time under the supplied mobility-proxy assumption.

Worked solution

The thickness ratio squared is one quarter. The coefficient ratio is \(\exp[(Q/R)(1/573.15-1/673.15)]\), since the Arrhenius prefactor cancels. Multiplication gives 1544.24. The reciprocal answers the opposite question. The formula additionally assumes a common thermodynamic factor and the same relaxation-mode geometry. Because the supplied constants are called host self-diffusion constants, interpreting them as B transport needs an explicit proxy assumption; the numerical ratio alone does not validate it.

5. What does a semicircle determine?

Derive the parallel RC circle and peak frequency. Does one fitted circle prove ionic bulk diffusion?

Worked solution

Set \(y=\omega R_eC_e\). Then \(\Re Z=R_e/(1+y^2)\) and \(-\Im Z=R_ey/(1+y^2)\). Eliminating y gives the circle centered at \(R_e/2\) with radius \(R_e/2\); its height peaks at y one. A fitted arc can constrain an effective resistance and time scale in this circuit model. Electrode, grain-boundary, or electronic responses can produce related shapes, so physical assignment needs geometry, temperature, frequency-range, and carrier evidence.

6. Local motion versus long-range diffusion

A loss peak persists when specimen thickness is changed, while another time grows as thickness squared. What interpretation is supported?

Worked solution

The thickness-independent time is compatible with local reorientation such as a Snoek process, provided the defect population and stress coupling remain comparable. The thickness-squared time supports a specimen-spanning diffusion mode such as Gorsky redistribution under fixed boundaries. Neither scaling alone uniquely identifies the microscopic species or jump network. An electrically active profile can supply complementary carrier evidence, while a tracer profile can test long-range tagged transport. Keep those observables distinct before combining them into a mechanism claim.

17.6 Sources and limits

L38 supplies the method sequence and L40 the worked input problems. The selected L40 frames at 16:47, 17:48, 18:48, and 19:49 establish the stated input values and the supplied Snoek relation. The rate-network derivation, explicit finite-strength loss convention, and RC calculation are original teaching developments.

Relaxation spectra and electrical signals can mix several processes. A single-time fit or equivalent circuit is a model for an observable, not proof that every measured event contributes to macroscopic mass transport. NMR next exposes that distinction through phase accumulation and local-field correlation times.

"""Mechanical and electrical probe checks with explicit conventions."""

import json
from pathlib import Path

import matplotlib
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import numpy as np

matplotlib.use("Agg")
matplotlib.rcParams["svg.hashsalt"] = "ch17"
BASE = Path(__file__).resolve().parents[1]
R = 8.31446261815324
a, tau_s = 0.287e-9, 1e-6
snoek_d = a * a / (36 * tau_s)
d0, activation = 2.3e-4, 280e3
temperatures = np.array([300, 400]) + 273.15
diffusivities = d0 * np.exp(-activation / (R * temperatures))
thickness = np.array([0.5e-3, 1e-3])
gorsky_times = thickness**2 / (np.pi**2 * diffusivities)
ratio = gorsky_times[0] / gorsky_times[1]
e0, einf = 100.0, 120.0
y = np.logspace(-2, 2, 1001)
eprime = einf - (einf - e0) / (1 + y * y)
edouble = (einf - e0) * y / (1 + y * y)
loss = edouble / eprime
expected = np.sqrt(e0 / einf)
assert abs(y[np.argmax(loss)] / expected - 1) < 0.01
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(9, 3.6))
ax[0].semilogx(y, edouble / (einf - e0), label="normalized modulus loss")
ax[0].semilogx(y, loss, label="loss tangent")
ax[0].set(
    xlabel="Angular frequency × stress-relaxation time",
    ylabel="Dimensionless response",
)
ax[0].legend(fontsize=8, loc="upper center", bbox_to_anchor=(0.5, 1.22))
theta = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 301)
resistance, capacitance = 1000.0, 1e-6
omega = np.logspace(-1, 7, 501)
z = resistance / (1 + 1j * omega * resistance * capacitance)
ax[1].plot(z.real, -z.imag)
ax[1].set(xlabel="Real impedance (ohm)", ylabel="Negative imaginary (ohm)")
ax[1].set_aspect("equal", adjustable="box")
fig.tight_layout()
fig.savefig(
    BASE / "assets/figures/ch17-relaxation.svg", metadata={"Date": None}
)
out = {
    "source_Snoek_D_m2_s": snoek_d,
    "Gorsky_host_mobility_proxy_assumption": True,
    "temperatures_K": temperatures.tolist(),
    "proxy_diffusivities_m2_s": diffusivities.tolist(),
    "proxy_Gorsky_times_s_Phi_equals_1": gorsky_times.tolist(),
    "time_ratio_sample1_over_sample2": float(ratio),
    "synthetic_modulus_loss_peak_omega_tau": 1.0,
    "synthetic_tan_delta_peak_omega_tau": float(expected),
    "synthetic_RC_peak_angular_frequency_rad_s": 1
    / (resistance * capacitance),
    "synthetic_RC_peak_negative_imaginary_ohm": resistance / 2,
}
