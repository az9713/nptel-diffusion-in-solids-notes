Two solutes in the same metal can differ enormously in mobility. Even one solute can move through several populations: substitutional atoms, interstitial atoms, trapped atoms, and atoms temporarily bound to a vacancy. A useful comparison must specify which population carries flux and which measured amount supplies the concentration gradient.

23.1 A diffusion comparison needs a reference state L49 00:20

Self-diffusion measures motion of atoms in a chemically uniform material, usually by an isotope tracer. Its tracer coefficient \(D^*\) has units m²s⁻¹ and describes spreading without a bulk chemical-composition gradient. An intrinsic coefficient describes a component flux in a specified frame, while an interdiffusion coefficient describes coupled composition evolution. Those quantities can differ because thermodynamic driving forces, vacancy flow, and correlations enter differently. A table that mixes them does not provide a controlled material ranking.

Absolute temperature \(T\), in kelvin, controls thermal activation. Homologous temperature \(T/T_m\) divides by a specified melting temperature \(T_m\). Comparing at equal \(T\) asks what happens in the same furnace; comparing at equal \(T/T_m\) asks about similar proximity to melting. These answer different questions. Alloy solidus and liquidus can differ, so even the normalization requires a convention.

Empirical trends across face-centered, body-centered, and close-packed hexagonal metals can organize observations, but packing fraction does not uniquely determine transport. A vacancy-mediated tracer coefficient contains vacancy population, jump mobility, jump geometry, and correlation. An apparently more open lattice can still have an unfavorable formation free energy or migration saddle. Source numerical melting-point tables are not reproduced here because their caption exponents are inconsistent; the chapter's numerical examples are independent constructions.

For a uniaxial crystal, symmetry permits \(\mathsf D=\operatorname{diag}(D_\perp,D_\perp,D_\parallel)\) in axes aligned with its special direction. A unit measurement direction \(\mathbf n\) has projected diffusivity \(D_n=\mathbf n^T\mathsf D\mathbf n\). If its angle to the axis is \(\vartheta\), this becomes \(D_\perp\sin^2\vartheta+D_\parallel\cos^2\vartheta\). Different exchange rates and correlations can contribute to the two tensor components. Structural and magnetic transitions can alter those ingredients; an Arrhenius fit spanning a transition should not be interpreted as one unchanged mechanism.

23.2 Competing defect channels produce an apparent enthalpy L49 08:31

Suppose independent monovacancy and divacancy channels contribute \(D_1,D_2\) to one tracer coefficient: \(D^*=D_1+D_2\). For dilute equilibrium vacancies, let \(H_f\) be the molar monovacancy formation enthalpy, \(H_b>0\) the energy released on binding two vacancies, and \(H_{m2}\) the divacancy migration enthalpy, all in Jmol⁻¹. Ignoring temperature-dependent prefactors and correlation derivatives, the divacancy channel has activation enthalpy

\[Q_2=2H_f-H_b+H_{m2}.\tag{23.1}\]

The formation of two vacancies costs \(2H_f\); favorable binding reduces the pair formation cost; migration adds its saddle cost. A larger equilibrium fraction of associated vacancies does not automatically make pairs the dominant diffusion carrier, because their migration rates and correlation efficiencies may differ. The population and the displacement per population are distinct factors.

For channels \(D_j=A_je^{-Q_j/(RT)}\) with positive constant prefactors \(A_j\), molar gas constant \(R\), and fixed \(Q_j\), differentiate their sum with respect to inverse temperature. Since \(dD_j/d(1/T)=-(Q_j/R)D_j\), the apparent activation enthalpy is

\[Q_{\mathrm{app}}=-R\frac{d\ln D^*}{d(1/T)}=\frac{\sum_jD_jQ_j}{\sum_jD_j}.\tag{23.2}\]

This is a contribution-weighted average, bounded by the smallest and largest channel enthalpies under these assumptions. Its weights change with temperature. Curvature in an Arrhenius plot can therefore arise from competing ordinary channels without requiring a single barrier to change continuously. Temperature-dependent correlations, prefactors, phase fractions, or defect sources require additional derivative terms and can invalidate the simple bounds.

The synthetic laboratory uses \((A_1,Q_1)=(10^{-5}\,\mathrm{m^2s^{-1}},180\,\mathrm{kJmol^{-1}})\) and \((A_2,Q_2)=(10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m^2s^{-1}},220\,\mathrm{kJmol^{-1}})\). At1000K, it obtains \(D_1=3.96231\times10^{-15}\) and \(D_2=3.22555\times10^{-15}\)m²s⁻¹, giving \(Q_{\mathrm{app}}=197.950\)kJmol⁻¹. A central finite difference in inverse temperature independently reproduces this slope to the printed precision.

23.3 Interstitial mobility and trapped storage L49 19:52

An interstitial solute occupies a site between the host lattice sites. In the dilute independent-jump limit, a direct interstitial does not need an equilibrium vacancy population before it can move. Its coefficient can therefore involve primarily migration, geometry, and correlation rather than the sum of vacancy formation and migration enthalpies. Setting tracer correlation factor to one additionally assumes statistically independent equivalent jumps and available destination sites. Exclusion, interactions, or traps can violate those assumptions even for the same chemical species.

Hydrogen illustrates why mobility, solubility, and apparent permeation must be separated. Lattice diffusivity describes motion of a mobile hydrogen population. Solubility describes how much is accommodated under specified chemical potential and phase conditions. A permeation experiment also involves entry and exit surfaces, trapping, specimen dimensions, and sometimes evolving microstructure. Rapid lattice motion alone cannot rank embrittlement severity, which depends on where hydrogen accumulates and how it interacts with stress and fracture processes. Isotope substitution can probe mass dependence, but a classical square-root mass argument is not universal when quantum effects or different trapping populations matter.

Consider an original dilute reversible-trap model. Let \(c_m\) and \(c_t\) be mobile and trapped molar concentrations. Suppose traps are immobile and equilibrate rapidly with \(c_t=Kc_m\), where constant dimensionless \(K\geq0\) includes trap density and association. The total is \(c_{\mathrm{tot}}=(1+K)c_m\), but only mobile atoms carry flux \(J=-D_m\partial_xc_m\). Conservation then gives

\[\partial_tc_{\mathrm{tot}}=\frac{D_m}{1+K}\partial_x^2c_{\mathrm{tot}},\qquad D_{\mathrm{app}}=D_m/(1+K).\tag{23.3}\]

The apparent coefficient is reduced by storage, not by an assumed reduction in every mobile jump rate. Synthetic \(D_m=10^{-9}\)m²s⁻¹ and \(K=99\) give \(D_{\mathrm{app}}=10^{-11}\)m²s⁻¹. Saturation makes \(c_t(c_m)\) nonlinear, slow exchange introduces an additional time scale, and irreversible trapping destroys the local-equilibrium reduction. Report which model a fitted coefficient represents.

23.4 Solubility and dilute-solute jump networks L51 00:21

A substitutional solid solution places solute atoms on host lattice sites; an interstitial solution places them between those sites. Solubility is an equilibrium phase-stability question. Hume–Rothery size, crystal-structure, electronegativity, and valence considerations are useful empirical heuristics for substitutional compatibility, not sufficient conditions for a stable phase. Full free energies and competing phases determine a phase boundary. Similarly, the largest empty geometric void need not be the lowest-free-energy interstitial site: local strain and chemical bonding matter. Carbon site preference in iron cannot be settled by drawing hard spheres alone.

A dilute impurity coefficient is defined by the limit of vanishing solute concentration in a specified host phase. “Impurity” does not require an unwanted addition or a universal concentration threshold. Dilution suppresses solute–solute interactions but leaves solute–vacancy binding and the correlated return of a neighboring vacancy.

For an FCC substitutional solute, the five-frequency model names five classes of vacancy exchange: \(\omega_0\) far from solute, \(\omega_1\) rotation within its neighbor shell, \(\omega_2\) direct solute exchange, \(\omega_3\) dissociation from the shell, and \(\omega_4\) association into it. Each rate is in s⁻¹ with its specified per-exchange convention. Solute transport depends on neighbor probability, exchange frequency, jump length, and the probability that subsequent motion undoes a preceding displacement.

Binding favors vacancy occupancy near the solute, but it can also favor repeated backtracking. The correlation factor depends on rate ratios, not just on binding free energy. At equilibrium, association and dissociation must satisfy detailed balance after neighbor multiplicities and state energies are included; the rates cannot be fitted as wholly unconstrained numbers. Thus stronger binding is not a general proof of faster tracer diffusion. Chapter8 develops the correlation structure; here the point is that equilibrium enrichment and transport efficiency play different roles.

Historical Lazarus–Le Claire electrostatic models approximate how a solute's valence difference changes vacancy interactions and saddle energies through a screened electronic potential. Effective charge in such a metallic model is not a literal unscreened ionic charge placed in a vacuum. Size effects, relaxation, transition-metal electronic structure, and prefactor changes can defeat a valence-only prediction. These models are useful as explicit hypotheses with limited chemical domains. A successful qualitative trend does not license extrapolation to every host or pressure range; no unverified source table is used here to assign numerical accuracy.

23.5 A tiny fast population can dominate flux L51 21:52

A hybrid solute can occupy substitutional and interstitial states. A schematic dissociative reaction is \(B_s\rightleftharpoons B_i+V\): a substitutional B atom leaves its lattice site to occupy an interstitial site, creating a vacancy. Proper mass action uses dimensionless activities and specified sublattice normalizations. A vacancy concentration is not interchangeable with the concentration of interstitial B atoms.

Let \(c_s,c_i\) be the two B concentrations and \(D_s,D_i\) their state-specific diffusion coefficients. Suppose exchange between states is rapid on the profile-evolution time scale, defects are buffered, and the fractions \(\theta_i=c_i/(c_s+c_i)\) and \(\theta_s=1-\theta_i\) are spatially constant. Add the two fluxes, substitute \(c_j=\theta_jc_{\mathrm{tot}}\), and obtain

\[J_B=-D_{\mathrm{hyb}}\partial_xc_{\mathrm{tot}},\qquad D_{\mathrm{hyb}}=\theta_iD_i+(1-\theta_i)D_s.\tag{23.4}\]

With synthetic \(\theta_i=10^{-6},D_i=10^{-8}\)m²s⁻¹ and \(D_s=10^{-17}\)m²s⁻¹, the result is \(1.00100\times10^{-14}\)m²s⁻¹. The interstitial population supplies approximately99.9001% of this coefficient while representing only one part per million of the solute. Spectroscopy that detects mostly substitutional B would therefore not establish that substitutional jumps dominate its flux.

If vacancy activity varies in space, the fractions can vary too. Differentiating \(c_i=\theta_i c_{\mathrm{tot}}\) then produces an extra term proportional to \(c_{\mathrm{tot}}\partial_x\theta_i\); (23.4) alone no longer describes the complete flux. Slow state exchange requires coupled reaction–diffusion equations. Semiconductor processing in Chapter25 makes these source and sink limitations especially consequential.

A dilute composition expansion is another local statement. With solute mole fraction \(N\), write \(D(N)=D(0)[1+b_1N+O(N^2)]\). The coefficient \(b_1\) is dimensionless for this fraction convention. An exponential fit \(D(0)e^{b_1N}\) has the same first derivative at zero but assumes particular higher-order terms. Synthetic \(b_1=5,N=0.02\) gives factors1.1 and1.10517. Agreement at small concentration is not evidence for the same broad-range physics.

23.6 Laboratory, exercises, and worked solutions

The executable laboratory evaluates the models in their stated units, differentiates the two-channel sum numerically, and writes actual output. The finite-difference apparent enthalpy differs from its analytic value by less than one part per million. This tests the mathematical reduction for synthetic constants; it does not validate a particular metal's mechanism.

  1. Pair activation. Compute \(Q_2\) for synthetic \(H_f=100,H_b=30,H_{m2}=60\)kJmol⁻¹.

    Solution

    Equation(23.1) gives \(2(100)-30+60=230\)kJmol⁻¹. Binding enters with a minus sign because it releases energy. Reversing the sign convention for binding without changing the equation would incorrectly increase the pair formation cost.

  2. Apparent-slope bounds. Prove the bounds for (23.2), then name a way they can fail experimentally.

    Solution

    The weights \(w_j=D_j/\sum_kD_k\) are positive and sum to one, so \(\min Q_j\leq\sum_jw_jQ_j\leq\max Q_j\). If a prefactor or correlation factor varies with temperature, its logarithmic derivative adds to the apparent slope. A structural transition or nonequilibrium defect population can likewise defeat the fixed-channel assumptions. A measured slope outside the proposed bounds rejects that simple model, not arithmetic itself.

  3. Trap saturation. If local equilibrium gives \(c_t=C_TK_ac_m/(1+K_ac_m)\), derive the differential capacity.

    Solution

    Here \(C_T\) is trap capacity in molm⁻³ and \(K_a\) has units m³mol⁻¹. Differentiate total concentration: \(dc_{\mathrm{tot}}/dc_m=1+C_TK_a/(1+K_ac_m)^2\). Conservation becomes this capacity times \(\partial_tc_m=D_m\partial_x^2c_m\). The capacity approaches one as traps saturate. It is generally incorrect to replace it by one fixed \(1+K\) over the whole concentration range.

  4. Hybrid dominance. Find the condition for the interstitial channel to contribute more than the substitutional channel.

    Solution

    Require \(\theta_iD_i>(1-\theta_i)D_s\), or \(\theta_i>D_s/(D_i+D_s)\). For a very large mobility contrast, this threshold can be extremely small. Dominance of the detected population and dominance of transport are therefore different questions.

  5. B2 diffraction check. For elastic scattering, let \(f_A,f_B\) be the complex single-atom scattering amplitudes, with \(i^2=-1\) defining the imaginary unit. At reciprocal vector \(\mathbf q=(2\pi/a_0)(h,k,l)\), with integer Miller indices \(h,k,l\) and cubic lattice parameter \(a_0\), an atom at fractional coordinate \(\mathbf r_j=(x_j,y_j,z_j)\) contributes amplitude \(f_j e^{2\pi i(hx_j+ky_j+lz_j)}\). Add these amplitudes for the B2 basis at0 and \((1/2,1/2,1/2)\), and identify a superlattice example.

    Solution

    The origin contributes \(f_A\); the body-center basis atom contributes \(f_Be^{i\pi(h+k+l)}\). Their sum is \(F_{hkl}=f_A+f_Be^{i\pi(h+k+l)}\). Even index sums give \(f_A+f_B\); odd sums give \(f_A-f_B\). The100 reflection can reveal chemical order when the species scatter differently. The110 index sum is even and is therefore allowed in the unlabeled body-centered structure, contrary to the captioned tutorial statement. With indistinguishable basis scattering, odd reflections vanish.

  6. Concentration units. Rewrite the dilute linear factor using atomic percent \(p=100N\).

    Solution

    Substitute \(N=p/100\) to obtain \(1+(b_1/100)p+O(p^2)\), with the higher-order coefficient also rescaled. Quoting the same numerical slope for a fraction and a percent creates a factor100 error. Always state which composition coordinate the derivative uses.

The models show why a single rate ranking is rarely a mechanism explanation. A useful interpretation specifies the carrier populations, their mobility and memory, the concentration being measured, and the state of the material during the experiment.
