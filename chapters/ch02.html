A coating equation without an initial state cannot predict a heat treatment. A specimen that already contains solute differs from an initially empty one, even if the same surface concentration is imposed afterward. A time transform makes that initial state appear explicitly in the equation to be solved.

2.1 A transform changes the time variable L4 04:25

An integral transform maps a function to weighted integrals of its values. Its kernel, integration interval, and convergence conditions define the particular transform. The one-sided Laplace transform is suited to a process specified from time zero onward. For a time-dependent function \(f(t)\), define

\[ F(s)=\mathcal L[f](s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt. \tag{2.1} \]

The transform variable \(s\) has units s⁻¹ when \(t\) is dimensional time, so the exponent is dimensionless. The value \(F\) has the units of \(f\) multiplied by time. We first use real positive \(s\); complex inversion extends it to a right half-plane, but the elementary inversions below can be checked directly without contour integration.

The improper integral means \(\lim_{T\to\infty}\int_0^T e^{-st}f(t)dt\). A sufficient existence condition is local piecewise continuity and exponential order: there are constants \(K,a,T_0\) with \(|f(t)|\leq Ke^{at}\) for \(t\geq T_0\). For real \(s>a\), the tail is bounded by \(K\int_{T_0}^\infty e^{-(s-a)t}dt\), which converges. Values on a finite interval remain integrable under the stated local condition.

A finite jump is allowed by piecewise continuity; an infinite one-sided limit is not. The tutorial function that equals \(1/(t-1)\) just to the right of \(t=1\) is not locally integrable there. Multiplication by the finite nonzero factor \(e^{-s}\) does not repair its logarithmically divergent integral. Thus convergence is a mathematical condition, not an optional formality suppressed by writing infinity as an integration endpoint.

For the constant function \(f=1\), direct integration gives \(F=1/s\) for \(s>0\). For \(f=e^{at}\), the integrand becomes \(e^{-(s-a)t}\), so \(F=1/(s-a)\) for \(s>a\). The sign of the exponent determines whether the discarded endpoint term actually vanishes. The same expression written outside its convergence domain is not the integral in (2.1).

Linearity follows from linearity of integration: \(\mathcal L[\alpha f+\beta g]=\alpha\mathcal L[f]+\beta\mathcal L[g]\) on a common convergence domain. The constants \(\alpha,\beta\) must not depend on time. Linearity will permit individual modes and forcing terms to be transformed separately. It does not make a nonlinear concentration-dependent diffusivity disappear.

2.2 Derivatives retain the initial data L4 14:49

Assume \(f\) is locally absolutely continuous: on each finite interval it equals its initial value plus the integral of an integrable derivative. Assume also that \(f'\) has a convergent transform in the domain used and \(e^{-sT}f(T)\to0\). Integration by parts on a finite interval gives \(\int_0^T e^{-st}f'(t)dt=[e^{-st}f(t)]_0^T+s\int_0^T e^{-st}f(t)dt\). Taking the limit produces

\[ \mathcal L[f']=sF-f(0),\qquad \mathcal L[f'']=s^2F-sf(0)-f'(0). \tag{2.2} \]

The second identity applies the first to \(f'\) and requires corresponding regularity and decay. The initial values are boundary terms at the lower integration limit. Dropping them changes an initial-value problem into a different problem. The dimensional check also fixes their position: \(sF\) and \(f(0)\) both have the units of \(f\).

The trigonometric transforms can be derived as a pair. For real frequency \(\omega>0\), let \(S=\mathcal L[\sin\omega t]\) and \(C=\mathcal L[\cos\omega t]\). Differentiation identities give \(sS=\omega C\) and \(sC-1=-\omega S\). Eliminating either unknown gives

\[ \mathcal L[\sin\omega t]=\frac{\omega}{s^2+\omega^2},\qquad \mathcal L[\cos\omega t]=\frac{s}{s^2+\omega^2},\quad s>0. \tag{2.3} \]

The numerator in the sine transform matters. The inverse of \(1/(s^2+\omega^2)\) is \(\sin(\omega t)/\omega\), not \(\sin(\omega t)\). This factor must be retained when checking a verbal or captioned inverse-transform expression. Units expose it: integrating a dimensionless sine over time must produce a quantity with units of time.

For nonnegative integer \(n\), repeated integration by parts gives another useful relation. The base case is \(\mathcal L[1]=1/s\). For \(n\geq1\), the endpoint at zero of \(t^n\) vanishes, and exponential decay removes the endpoint at infinity. Thus \(\mathcal L[t^n]=(n/s)\mathcal L[t^{n-1}]\), so

\[ \mathcal L[t^n]=\frac{n!}{s^{n+1}},\quad s>0. \tag{2.4} \]

The factorial counts the successive differentiation factors. The laboratory checks this identity by numerical quadrature for several powers rather than only consulting a symbolic transform table. Such finite checks can detect implementation mistakes; the integration-by-parts recurrence supplies the general result.

2.3 Initial-value problems and inverse transforms L4 18:53

The inverse transform recovers a time function with the given transform, within an appropriate uniqueness class. For the elementary examples here, partial fractions reduce the expression to transforms already derived. The proposed inverse is then checked against both the differential equation and all initial data. In this section and its ODE exercises, time and transform variables are nondimensional after choosing a reference time; numerical coefficients are not material rate constants.

First consider \(y''-y'-2y=0\) with \(y(0)=1\) and \(y'(0)=0\). A prime denotes a derivative with respect to this time variable. Let \(Y=\mathcal L[y]\). Substituting (2.2) gives \((s^2Y-s)-(sY-1)-2Y=0\). Therefore

\[ Y=\frac{s-1}{(s-2)(s+1)} =\frac{1}{3(s-2)}+\frac{2}{3(s+1)},\qquad y(t)=\frac13e^{2t}+\frac23e^{-t}. \tag{2.5} \]

At zero, the two contributions sum to one; their derivative contributions are \(2/3\) and \(-2/3\), giving zero. Each exponential independently solves the homogeneous equation. The growing term means the transform requires \(s>2\). An algebraic rational expression alone does not record this convergence condition unless we carry it with the solution.

The forced example is more sensitive to inverse-transform normalization. Solve \(y''+y=\sin2t\) with \(y(0)=2\) and \(y'(0)=1\). Transforming gives \((s^2+1)Y-2s-1=2/(s^2+4)\). Separation into elementary pieces yields

\[ Y=\frac{2s}{s^2+1}+\frac{5/3}{s^2+1}-\frac{2/3}{s^2+4}, \qquad y=2\cos t+\frac53\sin t-\frac13\sin2t. \tag{2.6} \]

The last coefficient is \(-1/3\) because the inverse of \(1/(s^2+4)\) contains a further factor \(1/2\). Direct differentiation gives \(y'(0)=5/3-2/3=1\). The homogeneous terms cancel in \(y''+y\), while the last term contributes \((-1/3)(-4+1)\sin2t=\sin2t\). Using \(-2/3\) for that coefficient fails both checks. The displayed source slide at L4 27:08 has the correct \(-1/3\) coefficient; the final captioned verbal correction is inconsistent with that slide.

A shifted quadratic denominator is handled by completing the square. Tutorial 1 asks for \(y''-3y'+4y=0\) with \(y(0)=1\) and \(y'(0)=5\). Its transformed numerator is \(s+2\), and the denominator is \((s-3/2)^2+7/4\). Split the numerator into \((s-3/2)+7/2\). Multiplying a time function by \(e^{at}\) shifts its transform from \(F(s)\) to \(F(s-a)\), directly from (2.1). Hence

\[ y(t)=e^{3t/2}\left[\cos\!\left(\frac{\sqrt7}{2}t\right) +\sqrt7\sin\!\left(\frac{\sqrt7}{2}t\right)\right]. \tag{2.7} \]

At zero, the bracket is one; its derivative is \(7/2\). The exponential adds \(3/2\), giving the required derivative five. The denominator has no real linear factors, but it has complex factors. Completing the square keeps the solution real and identifies its growth rate and oscillation frequency without claiming that factorization is impossible.

The laboratory uses exact symbolic differentiation to check the three ODE residuals and initial values. It also transforms the proposed inverse of a tutorial rational function back into the original expression. These checks target different failure modes: initial data can be satisfied by a function that has the wrong forcing, and a differential residual can vanish while the initial constants are wrong.

2.4 Differentiation under the integral sign L6 03:23

The concentration transform still depends on position. To transform a spatial derivative, we must know when differentiation and integration can be interchanged. Fixed integration limits remove endpoint-motion terms, but they do not remove the need for a convergence bound. A sufficient local condition is that the integrand is integrable at one base parameter in the neighborhood, has the relevant parameter derivative, and that this derivative is bounded throughout the neighborhood by an integrable function independent of that parameter. Base-parameter integrability is essential: a parameter-independent nonintegrable function has zero derivative but no finite integral to differentiate.

The reason is visible in the difference quotient. For a parameter \(\eta\) and integrand \(h(\eta,u)\), the mean-value theorem bounds \([h(\eta+\epsilon,u)-h(\eta,u)]/\epsilon\) by a nearby parameter derivative. An integrable common bound permits dominated convergence to pass the limit through the integral. Thus \(d\int hdu/d\eta=\int\partial_\eta hdu\) under these hypotheses. An improper integral without such control can invalidate the interchange.

The lecture's auxiliary example makes this operation concrete. Let \(u\in(0,1)\) and dimensionless parameter \(\eta>-1\). Define

\[ G(\eta)=\int_0^1\frac{u^\eta-1}{\ln u}\,du. \tag{2.8} \]

At \(u=1\), numerator and denominator both vanish, but their ratio tends to \(\eta\); this singularity is removable. Near zero, the stated range of \(\eta\) ensures integrability. Differentiating the integrand with respect to \(\eta\) gives \(u^\eta\), since the factor \(\ln u\) cancels. On any compact parameter interval with lower endpoint \(a>-1\), its magnitude is bounded by \(u^a\), whose integral is finite.

The justified interchange therefore gives \(G'(\eta)=\int_0^1u^\eta du=1/(\eta+1)\). Since \(G(0)=0\), integration with respect to the parameter yields \(G(\eta)=\ln(1+\eta)\). In particular, \(G(3)=\ln4\approx1.38629436112\). Independent numerical quadrature in the laboratory gives the same value within its integration error estimate.

This parameter is an auxiliary mathematical device, not the physical position in the coating. The distinction prevents a misleading unit assignment to \(u^\eta\). The calculation also shows why changing variables is sometimes less useful than introducing a parameter whose derivative simplifies the integrand. In the diffusion problem the desired interchange has the opposite purpose: it moves the spatial derivative outside the time integral.

2.5 Transforming Fick's second law L6 09:36

Return to dimensional position \(x\) in meters and time \(t\) in seconds. Let the nonreacting concentration satisfy \(\partial_t c=D\partial_{xx}c\) with constant \(D>0\), in m²/s. Write the initial concentration as \(c_0(x)=c(x,0)\) and define its one-sided time transform \(C(x,s)=\int_0^\infty e^{-st}c(x,t)dt\), in mol·s/m³.

Assume the required time boundary terms decay and weighted spatial derivatives admit integrable common bounds on each position neighborhood. Equation (2.2) transforms the time derivative into \(sC-c_0(x)\). The parameter-interchange argument transforms \(\partial_{xx}c\) into \(\partial_{xx}C\). Therefore

\[ D\,\partial_{xx}C-sC=-c_0(x),\qquad \partial_{xx}C-\frac{s}{D}C+\frac{c_0(x)}{D}=0. \tag{2.9} \]

This is an ordinary differential equation in position for each selected transform parameter. The initial spatial distribution becomes a forcing term. Its coefficient \(1/D\) in the divided form is essential: every term then has units mol·s/m⁵. The recorded derivation corrects this factor near L6 16:51. Keeping the undivided form alongside it provides a direct dimensional check.

If \(c_0=0\), the homogeneous solutions are \(e^{\pm kx}\), where \(k=\sqrt{s/D}\) has units m⁻¹. On a half-line \(x\geq0\) with bounded concentration as \(x\to\infty\), the growing exponential is excluded. If the surface is suddenly maintained at concentration \(c_s\), its transform is \(c_s/s\), yielding

\[ C(x,s)=\frac{c_s}{s}\exp\!\left(-x\sqrt{\frac{s}{D}}\right). \tag{2.10} \]

The boundary value applies for positive time; an initially empty interior can coexist with an abrupt boundary change at time zero in the usual limiting interpretation. The transform already displays a penetration length \(\sqrt{D/s}\). Chapter 3 derives its inverse and the associated \(\sqrt{Dt}\) spreading scale. No arbitrary constant remains after imposing boundedness and the surface condition.

A nonzero initial state requires a particular solution. For a uniform initially loaded half-line with concentration \(c_b\), \(C_p=c_b/s\) satisfies the inhomogeneous equation. A maintained surface \(c_s\) then gives \(C=c_b/s+(c_s-c_b)e^{-kx}/s\). Reusing the zero-initial-state homogeneous solution without this term would incorrectly erase the initial solute. The far-field transform must tend to \(c_b/s\), not zero.

A finite domain is also compatible with Laplace transformation. It has two spatial boundary conditions and generally retains both independent spatial solutions before those conditions are imposed. Eigenfunction expansions may expose long-time decay more transparently, but finite geometry does not make transforms inapplicable. What determines the physical solution is the domain, initial state, and boundary data together. The method must preserve all three.

2.6 Exercises with worked solutions

1. Convergence is part of the transform

Find the real convergence domain of the transform of \(e^{at}\), with real constant \(a\). Explain why a finite jump is compatible with existence but a positive \(1/(t-1)\) singularity to the right of one is not repaired by exponential weighting.

Worked solution

The tail integral is \(\int_0^\infty e^{-(s-a)t}dt\). It equals \(1/(s-a)\) when \(s>a\). At equality it integrates one forever; below equality it grows, so neither case converges. A bounded jump affects a finite integral on a finite interval. Near \(t=1\), however, the factor \(e^{-st}\) remains bounded above and below by positive constants. The singular part behaves as \(\int_1^{1+\epsilon}dt/(t-1)\), which diverges. A time-tail convergence factor does not remove a finite-time nonintegrable singularity.

2. Detect the missing frequency factor

For the forced initial-value problem (2.6), someone proposes \(2\cos t+(5/3)\sin t-(2/3)\sin2t\). Test its initial derivative and forcing. Give the corrected function and identify the exact transform-table mistake.

Worked solution

The proposed derivative at zero is \(5/3-4/3=1/3\), not one. Its second derivative plus itself is \((-2/3)(-4+1)\sin2t=2\sin2t\), twice the specified forcing. The transform coefficient \(-2/3\) multiplying \(1/(s^2+4)\) must be divided by the frequency two upon inversion. The corrected sine coefficient is \(-1/3\). It gives derivative one and forcing \(\sin2t\). Checking only \(y(0)=2\) would miss the error because both sine terms vanish there.

3. Recover the tutorial rational function

Invert \(F(s)=(8s^2-4s+12)/[s(s^2+4)]\). Show the coefficient comparison and verify the answer by taking its transform.

Worked solution

Write \(F=A/s+(Bs+C)/(s^2+4)\). Multiplying by the denominator gives \(8s^2-4s+12=(A+B)s^2+Cs+4A\). Thus \(A=3\), \(B=5\), and \(C=-4\). Inversion gives \(f(t)=3+5\cos2t-2\sin2t\). Its transform is \(3/s+5s/(s^2+4)-4/(s^2+4)\), exactly the original numerator over the common denominator. The code verifies this identity symbolically. No numerical coefficient from the inconsistent caption expression is accepted without the algebraic check.

4. A shifted quadratic and its initial data

Starting from \(Y=(s+2)/(s^2-3s+4)\), derive (2.7). Why is writing a numerator proportional only to \(s-3/2\) insufficient?

Worked solution

The denominator is \((s-3/2)^2+(\sqrt7/2)^2\). The numerator is \((s-3/2)+7/2\). The first part is a shifted cosine transform; the second is a shifted sine transform with coefficient \((7/2)/(\sqrt7/2)=\sqrt7\). This yields (2.7). Omitting the constant part would leave only \(e^{3t/2}\cos(\sqrt7t/2)\), whose derivative at zero is \(3/2\) instead of five. Completing the square locates the frequency, but matching the whole numerator supplies the initial derivative.

5. A parameter integral and an invalid interchange

Derive \(G(\eta)=\ln(1+\eta)\) from (2.8), naming a dominating function for the parameter derivative. What fails at \(\eta=-1\)?

Worked solution

On a compact parameter interval \([a,b]\subset(-1,\infty)\), \(\partial_\eta[(u^\eta-1)/\ln u]=u^\eta\leq u^a\) for \(0<u<1\). The integral of this bound is \(1/(a+1)\). Differentiation is justified and gives \(G'=1/(1+\eta)\). Integrating in the parameter and using \(G(0)=0\) gives the result. At minus one the bound would be \(1/u\), which is not integrable. The original integrand itself has a nonintegrable zero-endpoint behavior; the logarithm of \(1+\eta\) also diverges as this boundary is approached from above.

6. Preserve a nonzero initial state

A half-line initially has uniform concentration \(c_b\); its surface is maintained at \(c_s\). Derive the transformed solution and show that its far-field value has the correct units and initial-state interpretation. What happens when the two concentrations are equal?

Worked solution

The transformed equation is \(DC_{xx}-sC=-c_b\). The constant particular solution is \(c_b/s\). Boundedness removes the growing exponential, giving \(C=c_b/s+B e^{-x\sqrt{s/D}}\). At the surface, \(C=c_s/s\), so \(B=(c_s-c_b)/s\). The far field tends to \(c_b/s\), the transform of unchanged concentration \(c_b\), with concentration-times-time units. Equal concentrations remove the exponential term entirely; no gradient or transient is created. An initially empty formula would instead predict a spurious redistribution.

7. Substitution before integration by parts

Evaluate the tutorial integral \(\int t^7\sin(2t^4)dt\). Verify the result by differentiation rather than relying only on the integration steps.

Worked solution

Set \(u=t^4\), so \(du=4t^3dt\) and \(t^7dt=u\,du/4\). One integration by parts gives \(\tfrac14\int u\sin2u\,du=-u\cos2u/8+\sin2u/16+C\). Substitute back to obtain \(-t^4\cos(2t^4)/8+\sin(2t^4)/16+C\). Differentiation produces two opposite \(t^3\cos(2t^4)/2\) terms that cancel; the remaining term is \(t^7\sin(2t^4)\). The laboratory checks this cancellation exactly.

2.7 Sources and limits

L4, L5, and L6 supply the transform, ODE, and parameter-integral source spine. The original concentration boundary examples and explicit convergence/units checks are teaching developments. The mathematics is derived here and checked in the executable companion; no experimental diffusion coefficient is inferred in this chapter.

A transform is a solution method, not a physical closure. It preserves a specified linear diffusion model and its initial data. It does not select a microscopic mechanism, recover an unknown diffusivity from insufficient observations, justify an inconsistent boundary condition, or turn a nonlinear constitutive equation into a linear one automatically. Inverse formulas also require a suitable solution class; the direct checks here establish the stated elementary solutions under their explicit assumptions.
