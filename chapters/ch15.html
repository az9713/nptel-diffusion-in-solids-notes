An intermediate layer grows by receiving solute through material that it has already formed. The same layer that records progress also increases the transport resistance. A moving-boundary calculation must couple that changing diffusion field to the amount needed to transform the next increment of material.

Lectures 34–35 provide moving-interface and carburization demonstrations and a database tutorial. The mathematical Stefan problem below is an original reduced teaching calculation. It does not execute DICTRA, reproduce licensed databases, or represent measured Fe–C growth constants.

15.1 A moving front couples phase equilibrium to conservation L34 00:20

Consider planar product material occupying \(0<x<s(t)\) and an initially uniform parent material at \(x>s(t)\). The interface coordinate \(s\) is in meters, and \(v=ds/dt\) is positive for growth to the right. Let the selected conserved solute have molar concentration \(c(x,t)\) in the product, with constant diffusivity \(D>0\) in m²/s. Use a fixed laboratory coordinate and neglect bulk motion in this reduced equal-volume setting.

Impose a maintained surface concentration \(c_s\) at zero and a product-side interfacial concentration \(c_i\). The parent remains at \(c_0\) and is taken to have zero diffusive flux. Choose \(c_s>c_i>c_0\) so solute travels toward the advancing front. Local equilibrium is the assumption that fixes the two interfacial concentrations through thermodynamics; it is not derived from the diffusion equation. Define the positive concentration jump requirement \(q_c=c_i-c_0\), in mol/m³.

The general interfacial balance is \(v(c_R-c_L)=J_R-J_L\), with both fluxes in the same frame. Here \(c_L=c_i\), \(c_R=c_0\), and \(J_R=0\). Therefore

\[ q_c\frac{ds}{dt}=J_L=-D\left.\partial_xc\right|_{s^-}, \qquad c_t=Dc_{xx}\quad(0<x<s(t)). \tag{15.1} \]

The left side is the amount per area and time required for interface conversion. The right side is the flux supplied through the product. Their units both equal mol/(m²·s). A reaction that consumes a nonconserved chemical species would need its stoichiometric balance; the present component is conserved through phase conversion.

This formulation is a one-phase Stefan problem: a diffusion field and a moving boundary tied by a flux jump. “One-phase” refers to solving diffusion in only one region while the other supplies a fixed state, not to denying the existence of two physical phases. Including parent diffusion, unequal volumes, nonlocal equilibrium, or a finite surface supply changes the problem and its interface law.

15.2 Parabolic growth is a derived regime L34 12:39

First approximate the product concentration as quasisteady: diffusion across the current layer equilibrates much faster than the interface moves appreciably. Setting \(c_{xx}=0\) at fixed current thickness gives \(c=c_s-(c_s-c_i)x/s\). The flux is then \(J_L=D(c_s-c_i)/s\). Substitute this into (15.1) and define \(\beta=(c_s-c_i)/q_c>0\), a dimensionless concentration ratio:

\[ \frac{ds}{dt}=\frac{D\beta}{s},\qquad s^2(t)=s_0^2+2D\beta t. \tag{15.2} \]

The initial thickness \(s_0\) regularizes the zero-thickness singularity of this approximation. A prescribed seed states that product already exists; it does not model the probability or kinetics of nucleation. For negligible seed thickness, growth proportional to \(\sqrt t\) is called parabolic growth because thickness squared is linear in time.

The growing resistance \(s/D\) explains the slowing rate. A larger fixed concentration drop or diffusivity increases the parabolic constant; a larger concentration jump needed to convert parent to product decreases it. These are conditional comparisons with geometry, boundary states, and phase identities held fixed. An observed parabolic law alone does not identify which microscopic diffusion path controls D.

The approximation has an internal scale check. The diffusion time through the layer is approximately \(s^2/D\), while the interface growth time is \(s/(ds/dt)=s^2/(D\beta)\). Their ratio is \(\beta\). Thus small \(\beta\) supports quasisteady diffusion; values near one demand a transient calculation. This estimate explains why a linear profile can be useful in a slowly moving layer but inaccurate when its front advances on the diffusion time scale.

Neither (15.2) nor its similarity counterpart applies indefinitely to a finite closed specimen. As reservoirs deplete and the total phase fractions approach equilibrium, the driving concentration or potential difference changes. Growth can slow more strongly and saturate. Saturation in a numerical trajectory should be checked against conserved inventory and the equilibrium phase amounts, rather than identified from a flat curve alone.

15.3 An exact similarity solution tests the approximation

For the ideal zero-seed problem, a similarity solution retains concentration storage inside the growing product. Let \(s(t)=2\lambda\sqrt{Dt}\) with a constant dimensionless \(\lambda>0\). The error-function solution satisfying the two concentration boundaries is

\[ c(x,t)=c_s-(c_s-c_i) \frac{\operatorname{erf}[x/(2\sqrt{Dt})]}{\operatorname{erf}\lambda}, \quad 0<x<2\lambda\sqrt{Dt}. \tag{15.3} \]

Substituting into the diffusion equation confirms it directly. Differentiating at the front gives \(J_L=(c_s-c_i)\sqrt D e^{-\lambda^2}/[\sqrt{\pi t}\operatorname{erf}\lambda]\). The interface velocity is \(\lambda\sqrt D/\sqrt t\). Their equality in (15.1) reduces the moving-boundary problem to

\[ \lambda e^{\lambda^2}\operatorname{erf}\lambda =\frac{\beta}{\sqrt\pi}. \tag{15.4} \]

The left side increases from zero for positive lambda, so a unique positive root exists for each positive beta. For small lambda, \(\operatorname{erf}\lambda\approx2\lambda/\sqrt\pi\) and \(e^{\lambda^2}\approx1\), giving \(\lambda\approx\sqrt{\beta/2}\). Then \(s\approx\sqrt{2D\beta t}\), recovering the quasisteady law as a controlled small-beta limit.

The laboratory uses synthetic \(D=10^{-14}\) m²/s and \(t=10^5\) s. At beta 0.1, the computed root is 0.220016 and the exact layer thickness is 13.9151 micrometers, compared with 14.1421 micrometers quasisteady: a 1.632% overestimate. At beta one, the root is 0.620063 and thickness is 39.2162 micrometers versus 44.7214 micrometers, a 14.038% overestimate. Both follow square-root-time growth, so observing that exponent would not distinguish them.

The code brackets the positive root and checks (15.4), evaluates profiles and trajectories, and writes the saved output. A solver for more general initial data would additionally need conservative spatial discretization, interface tracking, and refinement checks. This exact case can serve as a benchmark for such a solver; plotting the analytic curve is not equivalent to having implemented that general solver.

Its phase-specific compositions, geometry, grids, thermodynamic database, mobility database, and thermal schedule are inputs, not details that can be inferred from the final line plot. Continuation to a longer annealing time retains an evolved spatial state. Restarting with a nominal average composition would instead pose a different problem.

Selected source screenshots establish an initially homogeneous Fe–0.15 mass% C composition, an isothermal condition of 1050 K, and a BCC ferrite seed with displayed width 0.001 micrometers, or one nanometer. The seed uses ten linear-grid points in the displayed setup. The plotted carbon-profile distance axis spans zero to 2000 micrometers and the displayed output times include zero, \(10^{-4}\) hours, and 300 hours. These are observed demonstration settings, not parameters of the synthetic solution in Figure 15.1.

A finite ferrite seed makes an initially absent region numerically available. It does not calculate ferrite nucleation, interface creation energy, or a nucleation delay. Similarly, local equilibrium at the moving boundary is a modeling choice. A finite interface mobility would relate chemical driving force to interface velocity and permit departure from the equilibrium boundary compositions. The reduced solution above holds those compositions fixed and cannot quantify that departure.

The source carburization example introduces another description: a local region can contain a matrix and carbides, with phase fractions changing across depth. A homogenized transport calculation assigns an effective response to that mixture. It is distinct from explicitly tracking every carbide interface. Effective-property bounds constrain possible responses under stated geometrical and constitutive assumptions; selecting an upper bound is a topology-sensitive modeling choice, not a measured diffusivity of every mixture.

The inspected multiphase setup uses a planar austenite region of width 0.003 m, or 3 mm, with 100 geometrically spaced points and a displayed spacing ratio of 1.02. Its selected phases are FCC_A1, M3C2, and M7C3. The source phase-fraction plot places the M3C2 region nearer the carbon-rich surface and M7C3 farther into the material. These labels are read from the screen; the calculation is not evidence that any unmodeled carbide must be absent in every real Ni–Cr–C alloy. The displayed homogenization choice is the general upper Hashin–Shtrikman bound. Selecting that option embodies an effective-medium assumption whose suitability must be checked against actual morphology.

An imposed carbon activity is also distinct from an imposed carbon concentration. Activity is defined through \(\mu_C=\mu_C^0+RT\ln a_C\). At fixed activity, the surface concentration follows from the selected thermodynamic phase state and can change when that state changes. The reference, such as graphite, is part of the boundary definition. A zero chromium flux boundary states a transport constraint on chromium; it is not a statement that chromium concentration is uniform throughout the evolving specimen.

15.5 Exercises with complete solutions

1. Recover the interface condition

Derive (15.1) from the general jump balance and explain its sign for an advancing solute-rich product.

Worked solution

Use \(v(c_R-c_L)=J_R-J_L\) with parent values on the right. The parent flux is zero, \(c_R=c_0\), and the product limit is \(c_L=c_i\). Thus \(v(c_0-c_i)=-J_L\), or \(q_cv=J_L\). With decreasing concentration toward the front, \(c_x<0\) and \(J_L=-Dc_x>0\), giving positive growth speed because \(q_c>0\). Using an unsigned concentration jump would obscure this directional check.

2. Derive and test the quasisteady law

Integrate the quasisteady growth equation with a finite seed. What dimensionless ratio supports the approximation?

Worked solution

The linear concentration profile gives \(ds/dt=D\beta/s\). Multiplying by twice s gives \(d(s^2)/dt=2D\beta\); integration gives \(s^2=s_0^2+2D\beta t\). Diffusion across the layer takes approximately \(s^2/D\), while appreciable interface motion takes \(s^2/(D\beta)\). Their ratio is beta. Small beta separates those time scales; beta near one warns that the neglected concentration storage can be consequential.

3. Obtain the exact root equation

Differentiate (15.3) and use the moving-front balance to obtain (15.4).

Worked solution

The derivative of the error function supplies \(e^{-\lambda^2}/\sqrt{\pi Dt}\) at the front. Hence \(J_L=(c_s-c_i)\sqrt D e^{-\lambda^2}/[\sqrt{\pi t}\operatorname{erf}\lambda]\). The speed from \(s=2\lambda\sqrt{Dt}\) is \(\lambda\sqrt D/\sqrt t\). Canceling the common factor in \(q_cv=J_L\) yields \(\lambda e^{\lambda^2}\operatorname{erf}\lambda=(c_s-c_i)/(q_c\sqrt\pi)\). The ratio on the right is beta divided by the square root of pi.

4. An exponent does not identify a model

Both the exact and quasisteady trajectories are proportional to square root of time. How can an experiment discriminate them?

Worked solution

The growth exponent is the same, so one must compare the prefactor using independently constrained D and concentration ratios, or compare internal concentration profiles. At beta one, the laboratory predicts 39.2162 micrometers exact and 44.7214 micrometers quasisteady at the declared time. Fitting an unconstrained effective D to either thickness can hide the difference. Multiple times do not necessarily break that degeneracy when both retain the same exponent; internal profiles or separate diffusivity evidence add information.

5. Activity versus concentration

Why can a fixed carbon activity boundary yield a changing surface concentration?

Worked solution

Fixed activity fixes chemical potential relative to a stated reference at the specified temperature. The concentration corresponding to that potential follows from the phase's thermodynamic constitutive function. If temperature, phase fractions, or surface phase identity changes, the concentration at the same activity can change. Imposing a fixed concentration instead constrains another variable and generally gives a different boundary problem. The reference chemical potential and thermal history must therefore accompany an activity boundary.

6. Diagnose a saturated interface

A closed-system simulation's interface appears to stop. What checks distinguish equilibrium saturation from numerical arrest?

Worked solution

Check total conserved component amounts against the initial inventory, local partition relations and equality of relevant chemical potentials, and final phase fractions against a separately calculated constrained equilibrium. Refine the mesh and time integration and tighten solver tolerances to test numerical sensitivity. Examine remaining gradients and fluxes. A seed reaching the end of a region or a failed nonlinear iteration can also stop apparent motion. A flat trajectory alone is insufficient evidence of equilibrium.
