A composition scan across an annealed junction shows slopes, plateaus, and abrupt steps. Before converting that scan into diffusivities, decide which features represent homogeneous material, interfaces, phase mixtures, and instrument averaging. The same-looking plateau can encode very different thermodynamic constraints.

Named experimental systems motivate categories; unresolved source micrograph labels are not used as quantitative evidence.

11.1 A profile has a domain, an amount balance, and a history L24 00:21

A composition profile assigns a measured or modeled composition to position in a specimen. Let \(x\) denote the coordinate normal to an initially planar junction and \(t\) the annealing time. Write \(z(x,t)=N_B\) for a dimensionless B mole fraction. Conversion to molar concentration requires the molar volume: \(c_B=z/V_m\), in mol/m³. A profile drawn in atomic percent, mass percent, or concentration is not interchangeable with a mole-fraction profile without the appropriate conversion.

The far-field behavior separates two physically different experiments. In a semi-infinite couple, the diffusion zone remains small relative to the distance to the outer faces, so each side retains its initial composition. The profile broadens while the remote reservoirs remain different. In a finite closed couple, material eventually reaches the outer faces; if one stable phase can hold the total composition, the long-time state becomes uniform. A semi-infinite solution cannot be extrapolated indefinitely to predict that final state.

For two finite blocks with amounts \(n_L,n_R\) and initial B fractions \(z_L,z_R\), conservation fixes the final overall fraction, regardless of diffusivity:

\[ \bar z=\frac{n_Lz_L+n_Rz_R}{n_L+n_R}. \tag{11.1} \]

Uniformity additionally requires that a homogeneous phase at this fraction be stable and kinetically accessible. If equilibrium instead contains two phases, the final overall fraction remains (11.1), while local compositions remain at the coexistence endpoints and phase fractions obey the lever rule. “Diffusion makes everything uniform” is therefore a statement about a particular stable single-phase model, not a general definition of diffusion.

The laboratory joins synthetic amounts two and one moles at fractions 0.2 and 0.8. Equation (11.1) gives 0.4, whereas the unweighted average gives 0.5. Equal geometrical lengths imply equal amounts only when cross-sectional areas and molar volumes support that inference. A composition line scan does not automatically reveal total amounts. An experimental comparison should record specimen dimensions, porosity, and possible evaporative or reactive losses before treating an average as a conserved value.

Within a stable solid solution, a smooth composition variation generally gives smoothly changing chemical potentials. For an ideal binary solution, \(\mu_B=\mu_B^0+RT\ln z\), with temperature \(T\), gas constant \(R\), and reference potential \(\mu_B^0\). The relation is logarithmic rather than universally proportional to composition. For a nonideal phase, the derivative of its Gibbs-energy function supplies the relation. A phase label must therefore accompany the profile when its shape is interpreted thermodynamically.

11.2 A jump is a boundary condition, not an enormous gradient L24 10:37

Suppose two phases meet at \(x=s(t)\). Their limiting concentrations are \(c_L=c_B(s^-,t)\) and \(c_R=c_B(s^+,t)\), each in mol/m³. The superscripts mean approach from the left and right. A locally equilibrated interface can have \(c_L\ne c_R\) while both component chemical potentials agree across it. The common tangent selects the two compositions. An actual finite-width atomic interface is here idealized as a mathematical surface.

Apply species conservation to a thin control interval that moves with that surface. Let \(J_L^{\rm lab},J_R^{\rm lab}\) be signed laboratory-frame fluxes immediately outside it, and let \(v=ds/dt\), in m/s. Relative to the moving interval, the flux is \(J^{\rm lab}-vc\). If the interface stores no finite excess amount and contains no reaction source for the selected conserved component, the relative incoming and outgoing fluxes agree. Thus

\[ J_L^{\rm lab}-vc_L=J_R^{\rm lab}-vc_R, \qquad v(c_R-c_L)=J_R^{\rm lab}-J_L^{\rm lab}. \tag{11.2} \]

This jump condition uses fluxes in one common frame. Substituting intrinsic lattice-frame fluxes without adding lattice motion can give a wrong interface velocity. If the concentration jump vanishes, (11.2) constrains flux continuity but does not determine velocity by division. If the interface stores an appreciable surface excess, its balance contributes an additional term.

For a synthetic jump from 10000 to 20000 mol/m³ with laboratory fluxes \(10^{-8}\) and \(3\times10^{-8}\) mol/(m²·s), (11.2) gives \(v=2\times10^{-12}\) m/s to the right. Both relative fluxes are \(-10^{-8}\) mol/(m²·s). The negative relative flux does not conflict with positive laboratory flux: the boundary overtakes some material as it moves. The laboratory checks the signed jump balance rather than comparing magnitudes alone.

A finite experimental probe convolves the true profile with its spatial response. It can round a composition discontinuity into a steep continuous-looking transition. Dividing a flux by that measured slope would assign a coefficient to the instrument-blurred interface, not to either homogeneous phase. Identify phase boundaries before applying a single-phase inverse formula. The spatial jump and the corresponding chemical-potential continuity answer different questions.

11.3 Narrow phases and changing phase fractions L24 14:41

An intermediate phase can occupy a substantial spatial layer while admitting only a narrow homogeneity range: the composition interval over which its homogeneous structure exists under specified conditions. The width of that interval is dimensionless; the thickness of its layer is a length. A broad layer with an almost flat composition profile therefore does not imply a broad phase field in the phase diagram.

Let the exchange potential change by \(\Delta\mu\) across such a layer while composition changes by only \(\Delta z\). A large derivative \(d(\mu_B-\mu_A)/dz\) permits a finite potential variation with a small composition change. The flux law based on potential can remain finite even though a composition-based coefficient becomes very large or poorly identifiable. In the exact line-compound idealization, composition is fixed; a gradient inferred by dividing two unresolved numbers has no pointwise meaning. Integrated diffusion measures or explicit defect/mobility models are needed.

Figure 11.1 compares two original piecewise profiles with the same chosen spatial layer width and different allowed composition ranges. The drawing specifies no annealing time or material coefficient; it illustrates the independent meanings of spatial thickness and compositional width. Phase identification in a real sample also needs evidence such as diffraction, crystallography, and calibrated composition. Image contrast or a flat segment alone is not sufficient.

A different kind of apparent plateau is an equilibrium two-phase mixture. At fixed temperature and pressure in a binary system, the endpoint compositions and both chemical potentials are fixed along one tie-line. Two specimens can have different overall compositions solely because their phase fractions differ. Joining them supplies no chemical-potential difference if their phases are already in equilibrium on that same tie-line. There can be a visible difference in phase fraction without a driving force for bulk chemical interdiffusion in this ideal construction.

This observation requires the same tie-line, the same temperature and pressure, and no additional driving fields. Regions in different two-phase fields need not have the same potentials. Interface curvature, stored strain, or an externally imposed field can also drive redistribution. A statement about zero chemical force in a specified equilibrium state is not a general claim that mixtures never evolve.

11.4 Diffusion paths, incremental couples, and spectators L26 00:21

A diffusion path is the sequence of local compositions encountered while moving through a diffusion zone, represented in composition space. In a binary system it lies on a line because \(N_A+N_B=1\). Its phase jumps skip compositions that are not occupied as a homogeneous phase. In a ternary or higher-component system, the path can curve: relative fluxes and kinetic cross coupling change local component ratios. A straight line joining end-member compositions need not be the actual path.

Experimental systems in the source span broad solid solutions, narrow intermetallics, and multiple reaction layers. They include Au–Cu, Ni–Sn, W–Zr, Mg–Al, Ag–Zn, Ni–Al, and several silicide couples. Their common teaching role is to break the inference from phase-diagram width to measured layer thickness. Exact reported temperatures, widths, and phase labels require the corresponding original micrograph and attribution; the schematic reasoning here does not reproduce those empirical measurements or infer unread stoichiometries.

An incremental couple joins compositions chosen close together within a target phase. For example, compositions on either side of an ordered alloy's reference composition can restrict the observed zone to a small portion of its homogeneity range. This makes a local effective interdiffusion coefficient easier to interpret, provided the phase remains single and the difference is large enough to measure accurately. The approximation is a local linearization of a constitutive response; it does not prove that diffusivity is composition-independent over the full phase field.

A pseudobinary couple is a multicomponent construction in which only two components vary appreciably through the diffusion zone. The other components are spectators whose profiles must remain uniform. The source end members have nominal percentages Cr25Fe25Co20Ni30 and Cr25Fe25Co30Ni20. The active Co and Ni fractions sum to 0.5, so a normalized Co coordinate for the active subspace is \(Y_{\rm Co}=N_{\rm Co}/(N_{\rm Co}+N_{\rm Ni})\), varying initially from 0.4 to 0.6. This normalization is useful only after the spectator constraint is verified throughout the zone.

Equal spectator endpoints are necessary but not sufficient. In a multicomponent kinetic law, a spectator flux can be driven by the other chemical-potential gradients. A uniform initial spectator concentration can therefore redistribute internally while retaining equal distant endpoint values. The right panel of Figure 11.1 constructs \(N_s(\xi)=0.25+0.02\sin^3(\pi\xi)\) on \(-1\leq\xi\leq1\), where \(\xi=x/L_{\rm ref}\) and \(L_{\rm ref}>0\) is a reference length. Both endpoints are 0.25, the minimum is 0.23 at \(\xi=-1/2\), and the maximum is 0.27 at \(\xi=1/2\). The deviation is odd, so its integral over the interval is zero. With the same constant molar volume as the initial state, the depletion exactly balances the excess and spectator inventory is conserved. This schematic is a counterexample to endpoint sufficiency; it is not a solved multicomponent diffusion history or a measured alloy profile.

A credible pseudobinary analysis measures every component, checks the spectator profiles against uncertainty, establishes phase identity and molar-volume conventions, and then applies the reduced inverse model only within its verified domain. If a spectator hump exceeds measurement uncertainty, a binary coefficient may no longer represent the underlying transport matrix. Calling an alloy “high entropy” does not remove these constraints; the local path and the observation model still determine which parameters are identifiable.

11.5 Tutorial checks: missing zones and the dilute limit L25 00:21

A binary single-phase profile is commonly described by one interdiffusion coefficient function \(\widetilde D(z)\), whereas its constituents have separate intrinsic coefficients relative to the lattice. “One coefficient” means one constitutive function for the constrained binary profile, not one universal number for every composition and temperature. Constant \(\widetilde D\) remains a legitimate approximation; the fact that concentration varies does not logically force that coefficient to vary.

A specimen with no visible interdiffusion zone admits several explanations. The chemical potentials could already agree, the diffusion length could be smaller than the resolution, or the anneal could be too short. Poor contact and an oxide barrier add experimental possibilities. A null profile alone does not select among them. Varying annealing time, improving spatial resolution, and inspecting the interface answer different parts of the diagnosis. A square-root-time spreading law is a prediction under a fixed diffusion model, not an all-purpose test for every reaction zone.

The tutorial's mixing-energy calculation compares a mixed value of 3 J/mol with an unmixed reference of 8 J/mol, giving \(\Delta g=-5\) J/mol. If the mixing enthalpy is zero and the mixing entropy is 5 mJ/(mol·K), conversion to 0.005 J/(mol·K) gives \(T=-\Delta g/\Delta s=1000\) K. The sign and the milli-prefix both matter. A reported negative mixing energy supports mixing relative to that reference, but phase stability still requires comparison with every allowed competing state.

The regular-solution derivative is \(dg_{\rm mix}/dz=\Omega(1-2z)+RT\ln[z/(1-z)]\). It tends to negative infinity as \(z\to0^+\) at positive temperature with finite \(\Omega\). Nevertheless \(g_{\rm mix}\to0\), because both \(z\ln z\) and \(z(1-z)\) vanish. A diverging slope at a boundary does not mean an infinite finite-composition energy change. Integrating the slope over a shrinking interval gives a shrinking change.

Consequently this mathematical limit does not, by itself, prove that a finite isolated pure specimen is impossible or determine its impurity content. Impurity exchange requires a reservoir chemical potential, allowed defect states, and a finite-system or thermodynamic-limit specification. The logarithmic ideal activity tells how the continuum model responds near dilution; a claim about achievable purity adds a physical preparation problem.

The laboratory takes fixed synthetic concentrations, fluxes, and endpoint fractions. It writes Figure 11.1 and the numerical record, checking the signed interface balance and constructing the spectator counterexample. It does not fit raw microscopy or produce a phase identity. A user replacing the synthetic data must supply a common flux frame and concentrations, not just percentages with unspecified volume conversion.

11.6 Exercises with complete solutions

1. Finite and semi-infinite couples

Two and one moles of a stable binary solution start at B fractions 0.2 and 0.8. Find their closed-system final composition. What changes if the same initial fractions represent two semi-infinite reservoirs?

Worked solution

The B amount is \(2(0.2)+1(0.8)=1.2\) moles in three total moles, giving 0.4. For semi-infinite reservoirs the total amounts are not finite inputs to this formula. At any finite time the far-field compositions remain 0.2 and 0.8 under that idealization, while a diffusion zone broadens between them. There is no finite global homogeneous composition supplied by averaging two infinite reservoirs. A finite specimen eventually leaves that approximation when its outer boundaries become influential.

2. Check an interface sign

Use the concentrations and fluxes in Section 11.2 to calculate both moving-frame fluxes and the interface velocity. What error results from using only the larger laboratory flux?

Worked solution

The flux difference is \(2\times10^{-8}\) and the concentration jump is \(10^4\), so \(v=2\times10^{-12}\) m/s. On the left, subtracting \(vc_L=2\times10^{-8}\) from \(10^{-8}\) gives \(-10^{-8}\). On the right, subtracting \(vc_R=4\times10^{-8}\) from \(3\times10^{-8}\) gives the same result. Using only the right flux ignores solute supplied or removed on the left and violates the interface balance.

3. A two-phase couple with no chemical force

Two equilibrated binary mixtures occupy the same tie-line but have different overall compositions. Explain how their chemical potentials can agree and their microscopy images can differ.

Worked solution

At fixed temperature and pressure the two endpoint phase compositions are fixed by equality of both component potentials. Each mixture is assembled from those same phases, so each component has the same potential in either specimen. Changing overall composition changes the lever-rule phase fractions. Different phase fractions can alter image contrast or connectivity without changing the equilibrium potentials. The conclusion requires a common tie-line and absence of other driving fields; it cannot be extended to arbitrary mixtures with different coexistence endpoints.

4. Endpoints do not establish a pseudobinary path

Construct a spectator fraction with equal endpoint values but nonuniform interior, conserving its inventory relative to an initially uniform 0.25 fraction under constant molar volume. State what data would make a pseudobinary reduction defensible.

Worked solution

On \(-1\leq\xi\leq1\), use \(0.25+0.02\sin^3(\pi\xi)\). The sine vanishes at both ends. Its cube is −1 at \(\xi=-1/2\) and +1 at \(\xi=1/2\), giving minimum 0.23 and maximum 0.27. Because \(\sin^3(\pi\xi)\) is odd, its integral over this symmetric interval is zero. Multiplication by the common spatial scale and inverse molar volume preserves zero excess amount. Equal endpoints therefore coexist with redistribution and conserved inventory. A defensible reduction needs measured profiles for all spectators that are uniform within quantified uncertainty, plus a phase-consistent active-component path and appropriate concentration/volume conventions. Equal nominal starting compositions alone provide none of the interior information.

5. Divergent derivative, finite energy

Explain how \(d(z\ln z)/dz\) can diverge at zero while \(z\ln z\) tends to zero. What does this imply for the tutorial purity argument?

Worked solution

The derivative is \(1+\ln z\), which diverges negatively. Setting \(z=e^{-u}\) with \(u\to\infty\) gives \(z\ln z=-ue^{-u}\to0\). The integral of the logarithmic singularity over a shrinking interval is finite and tends to zero. A large local derivative is therefore compatible with a small total energy change. The derivative alone cannot determine finite specimen impurity content; reservoir and defect assumptions must be supplied.

6. Interpret a narrow plateau

A thick layer has a composition variation smaller than the probe uncertainty. Can one conclude that diffusion within it is zero? Design a next measurement.

Worked solution

No. A narrow homogeneity range can support a finite chemical-potential gradient and finite flux while its concentration variation is unresolved. Instrument convolution can also hide a small gradient. First establish phase identity and calibrate the composition resolution; use independent diffusion or tracer measurements, repeated-time layer growth, or an integrated-flux model to constrain transport. Dividing an inferred flux by a slope statistically indistinguishable from zero yields an unstable coefficient, not evidence of zero transport. A line-compound treatment should use its own conserved-flux and defect model.
