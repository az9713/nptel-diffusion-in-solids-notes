A thin tracer deposit and a maintained carburizing atmosphere do not supply the same amount of solute. The tracer deposit is finite; the atmosphere can keep adding material. Their profiles have different amplitudes and amount balances, even when the solid has the same constant diffusivity. Selecting a formula by its shape alone can therefore give a wrong treatment time or inferred coefficient.

3.1 Specify the source before solving the equation L7 00:20

Use one-dimensional, nonreacting diffusion with uniform constant \(D>0\), in m²/s. Position \(x\) is measured in meters, time \(t>0\) in seconds, concentration \(c\) in mol/m³, and signed flux is \(J=-D\partial_xc\). The governing equation is \(\partial_tc=D\partial_{xx}c\). Its domain and source specification will now change while this local equation remains fixed.

An instantaneous plane source deposits a finite amount per area at one location and then supplies nothing further. A maintained surface fixes a concentration while allowing the total supplied amount to grow. A diffusion couple initially joins two regions with different uniform concentrations. In an effectively infinite couple, each side contains an unbounded reservoir in the mathematical approximation; only the amount transferred across a specified plane over a finite time is finite.

The infinite-domain approximation is useful when real remote boundaries lie many diffusion lengths away from the region observed. The relevant length will be proportional to \(\sqrt{Dt}\). Increasing annealing time eventually makes a previously remote boundary important. There is no universal numerical definition of “semi-infinite” independent of the desired error tolerance and measurement location.

For comparison, the steady planar membrane from L7 has endpoints \(c_1,c_2\) over thickness \(L\). Chapter 1 gives \(c(L/3)=(2c_1+c_2)/3\) and \(J=D(c_1-c_2)/L\). Solving that steady boundary-value problem needs no initial profile. A transient problem does. Boundary conditions may themselves vary with time; being prescribed does not make them constant.

3.2 A finite plane dose produces a Gaussian L7 10:33

Let \(M>0\), in mol/m², be the deposited amount per area. Put the source at the origin in an effectively infinite solid. The total amount condition is \(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}c(x,t)dx=M\). An infinitely thin initial deposit is represented as \(c(x,0)=M\delta(x)\), where the Dirac delta is defined through \(\int\delta(x)\varphi(x)dx=\varphi(0)\) for a smooth test function \(\varphi\). It is not an ordinary finite concentration at the origin.

Seek an even, smooth spreading profile with the conserved amount and no independent length scale. Define \(\xi=x/(2\sqrt{Dt})\) and write \(c=M(Dt)^{-1/2}F(\xi)\). The amplitude factor preserves the possibility of a constant integral while width grows. Substitution into the diffusion equation gives

\[ F''+2\xi F'+2F=0, \qquad (F'+2\xi F)'=0. \tag{3.1} \]

Primes denote derivatives with respect to \(\xi\). Even symmetry gives \(F'(0)=0\), so the integrated constant is zero. Solving \(F'=-2\xi F\) gives \(F=Ae^{-\xi^2}\). Normalization requires \(2A\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-\xi^2}d\xi=1\). The Gaussian integral is \(\sqrt\pi\): squaring the integral and using polar coordinates gives \(\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2}rdrd\theta=\pi\). Hence

Derived solution 3.1 — infinite plane source. \[ c(x,t)=\frac{M}{\sqrt{4\pi Dt}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{x^2}{4Dt}\right). \tag{3.2} \] The source is a plane in a three-dimensional specimen, uniform along the plane; \(M\) is an areal amount, not total moles from a three-dimensional point source.

Three checks give the formula physical meaning. Its prefactor has units \((\mathrm{mol/m^2})/\mathrm m=\mathrm{mol/m^3}\). Its integral is \(M\). Its normalized second moment is \(M^{-1}\int x^2c\,dx=2Dt\), as follows by the same substitution and one integration by parts. The first moment is zero by symmetry. Thus width grows like the square root of time even though the mean position remains fixed.

The singular initial state is recovered in an integral sense. Substitute \(x=2\sqrt{Dt}\xi\) into \(\int c(x,t)\varphi(x)dx\). It becomes \((M/\sqrt\pi)\int e^{-\xi^2}\varphi(2\sqrt{Dt}\xi)d\xi\), which tends to \(M\varphi(0)\) for bounded continuous test functions by dominated convergence. Setting the initial concentration equal to zero everywhere would instead lose the entire dose.

3.3 Surface tracers and slope measurements L7 25:53

A thin source deposited at the surface of a half-space \(x\geq0\) has a different normalization. After deposition, assume no further supply and no loss through the exposed surface. The boundary is reflecting: \(\partial_xc(0,t)=0\). Restrict the even Gaussian to positive positions and double it, so its half-line integral remains \(M\):

\[ c(x,t)=\frac{M}{\sqrt{\pi Dt}}e^{-x^2/(4Dt)},\qquad x\geq0. \tag{3.3} \]

The zero surface derivative verifies the boundary condition; doubling the amplitude supplies the lost negative half of the integral. This is a finite-dose experiment, unlike a maintained nonzero surface concentration. A maintained surface keeps its concentration fixed, whereas the finite-dose surface value in (3.3) decays as \(t^{-1/2}\).

At a known annealing time, natural logarithms give \(\ln c=\ln[M/\sqrt{\pi Dt}]-x^2/(4Dt)\). If a measured activity is proportional to concentration by a position-independent factor, the proportionality changes the intercept but not the slope. A graph of natural-log activity against squared depth therefore has slope \(m=-1/(4Dt)\), giving \(D=-1/(4tm)\). For base-ten logarithms the slope is instead \(-1/(4Dt\ln10)\).

A straight line is evidence compatible with the model, not proof of a unique atomistic mechanism. Initial source thickness, section averaging, instrument resolution, surface loss, multiple paths, and background can alter the profile. A finite-thickness Gaussian initial layer adds its initial variance to \(2Dt\); confusing that width with diffusion alone biases an inferred coefficient. Chapter 16 develops measurement broadening explicitly.

For (3.3), the normalized second moment about the surface is still \(2Dt\), but the mean depth is positive, \(2\sqrt{Dt/\pi}\). The centered variance is therefore \(2Dt(1-2/\pi)\). A half-Gaussian surface profile and a whole-line Gaussian have different centered-variance conventions. Reporting merely a “width” without defining it can conceal this distinction.

3.4 A maintained surface gives an error-function profile L8 00:19

Now let a half-space initially have uniform concentration \(c_0\), with maintained surface value \(c_s\ne c_0\) and far-field value \(c_0\). Define normalized excess \(\theta=(c-c_0)/(c_s-c_0)\). Its boundary value is one and its initial interior value is zero. Seek \(\theta=F(\xi)\) with the same \(\xi=x/(2\sqrt{Dt})\). Chain differentiation yields \(F''+2\xi F'=0\).

Integrate once: \(F'=Ae^{-\xi^2}\). The two boundary conditions require \(F(\xi)=-A\int_\xi^\infty e^{-u^2}du\) and \(-A\sqrt\pi/2=1\). Define the error function and its complement by

\[ \operatorname{erf}z=\frac{2}{\sqrt\pi}\int_0^z e^{-u^2}du, \qquad \operatorname{erfc}z=1-\operatorname{erf}z. \tag{3.4} \]

The error function is odd because its integrand is even. It is zero at zero and tends to one at positive infinity. Its derivative \(2e^{-z^2}/\sqrt\pi\) is positive for every finite real \(z\), so it has a unique real inverse on \((-1,1)\). The normalized solution is \(F=\operatorname{erfc}\xi\), giving

\[ c(x,t)=c_0+(c_s-c_0)\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right). \tag{3.5} \]

This independently derives the inverse of the half-line transform in Chapter 2. At the surface the complement is one; far away it tends to zero. At each fixed positive depth it tends to zero as time approaches zero, recovering the initial interior state. The incompatibility of the initial interior concentration and changed boundary at the single corner \((0,0)\) is part of the ideal sudden-switch model.

Differentiate (3.5). The inward surface flux is \(J(0,t)=(c_s-c_0)\sqrt{D/(\pi t)}\). Integrating the excess concentration over the half-line gives uptake per area

\[ M_{\rm excess}(t)=2(c_s-c_0)\sqrt{\frac{Dt}{\pi}}. \tag{3.6} \]

One derivation uses \(\int_0^\infty\operatorname{erfc}u\,du=1/\sqrt\pi\), obtained by integration by parts. Differentiating (3.6) then reproduces the surface flux, a separate global balance check. A negative concentration difference gives outward loss instead of inward uptake. The initial \(t^{-1/2}\) flux singularity is integrable: finite time supplies a finite amount. Real surface-transfer kinetics can regularize this short-time idealization.

A constant gas flow does not by itself establish a fixed solid-surface concentration. Surface reactions, gas-to-solid transfer, and equilibrium partitioning may impose a flux or mixed boundary condition instead. Likewise, composition-dependent diffusivity can change the error-function shape. The profile is exact for the stated scalar constant-diffusivity boundary problem, not a universal carburizing law.

3.5 A diffusion couple is a superposition of plane sources L8 10:33

On the whole line let the initial concentration be \(c_L\) for \(x<0\) and \(c_R\) for \(x>0\). The values \(c_L,c_R\) are constant end-member concentrations in the same convention. Subtract the uniform right-side baseline. The initial excess on each infinitesimal source slice at position \(y<0\) is \((c_L-c_R)dy\), an areal amount.

Linearity permits these elementary sources to be added. With the normalized Gaussian kernel \(G(x,t)=e^{-x^2/(4Dt)}/\sqrt{4\pi Dt}\), the excess at observation position \(x\) is \((c_L-c_R)\int_{-\infty}^0G(x-y,t)dy\). The integration coordinate \(y\) labels source position; it is not the observation coordinate. Substitute \(u=(x-y)/(2\sqrt{Dt})\) to obtain

\[ c(x,t)=c_R+\frac{c_L-c_R}{2}\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right) =\frac{c_L+c_R}{2}-\frac{c_L-c_R}{2}\operatorname{erf}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right). \tag{3.7} \]

At negative and positive infinity the solution approaches the respective end members. At the original contact plane it equals their mean. The midpoint equality is a result of this constant-coefficient symmetric scalar model. Real binary interdiffusion with composition-dependent coefficients or unequal partial volumes need not keep a fixed arithmetic midpoint at the original plane.

The lecture motivates a dilute species moving much faster than its host. That is one physical route to a simple scalar approximation, not a mathematical requirement that all diffusion couples obey an inequality between two atom-specific diffusivities. Equation (3.7) assumes a single constant coefficient for the represented concentration field. Chapter 13 distinguishes the interdiffusion coefficient governing a binary composition profile from the individual intrinsic and tracer coefficients.

The flux through the original plane is \((c_L-c_R)\sqrt D/(2\sqrt{\pi t})\). Integrating it from zero to time \(t\) gives transferred amount \((c_L-c_R)\sqrt{Dt/\pi}\) per area. This equals the excess on the initially right side, found by integrating (3.7) relative to \(c_R\). Although the total amount on an infinite line may diverge when the end members are nonzero, the transferred excess is finite and has a well-defined balance.

Figure 3.1 compares the three source classes. Let an arbitrary reference length be \(\ell_0>0\) and define plotted position \(X=x/\ell_0\) and time \(\tau=Dt/\ell_0^2\). The finite-dose peak falls as it widens. The diffusion-couple midpoint stays fixed. The maintained surface keeps its boundary value fixed while uptake grows.

3.6 An imposed flux supplies a fourth boundary model

As an original extension, impose a constant inward flux \(q\), in mol/(m²·s), on an initially uniform half-space of concentration \(c_0\). The boundary condition is \(-D\partial_xc(0,t)=q\). The transformed excess solves the zero-initial-state homogeneous spatial equation; its boundary derivative fixes the amplitude as \(q/[s\sqrt{Ds}]\), rather than \((c_s-c_0)/s\).

A direct construction avoids needing a new transform table. The spatial derivative \(w=\partial_xc\) itself solves the constant-coefficient diffusion equation, has zero initial interior value, and maintained boundary \(w(0,t)=-q/D\). Equation (3.5) therefore gives \(w=-(q/D)\operatorname{erfc}[x/(2\sqrt{Dt})]\). Integrate from position \(x\) to infinity, using \(c\to c_0\), to find

\[ c-c_0=\frac qD\left[\frac{2\sqrt{Dt}}{\sqrt\pi}e^{-x^2/(4Dt)} -x\operatorname{erfc}\!\left(\frac{x}{2\sqrt{Dt}}\right)\right]. \tag{3.8} \]

The bracket is the integral of a nonnegative complement and is therefore nonnegative for an inward positive flux. At the surface, concentration grows as \(c(0,t)-c_0=2q\sqrt{t/(\pi D)}\). Integrating the amount balance gives excess \(qt\) per area, because the imposed entry rate is constant. A fixed-flux surface and a fixed-concentration surface cannot generally be imposed simultaneously; their time-dependent concentration/flux responses differ.

3.7 Infer a depth or coefficient with a controlled inverse L10 09:52

A case depth must name a concentration threshold. For (3.5), let \(c_*\) lie strictly between the initial and surface values. Define the normalized deficit \(r=(c_s-c_*)/(c_s-c_0)\in(0,1)\). The target depth satisfies \(\operatorname{erf}[x_*/(2\sqrt{Dt})]=r\). Its unique positive inverse gives

\[ x_*=2\sqrt{Dt}\operatorname{erf}^{-1}(r),\qquad t=\frac{x_*^2}{4D[\operatorname{erf}^{-1}(r)]^2}. \tag{3.9} \]

The second form is appropriate when choosing a treatment duration. With the same coefficient and concentration threshold, doubling depth requires four times the duration. This scaling is exact within the model and independent of the particular inverse-function value. It fails if temperature, diffusivity, surface conditions, phase state, or target convention changes with the treatment.

The caption-stated tutorial inputs \(c_0=0.25\), \(c_s=1.2\), \(c_*=0.8\) in a common mass-percent convention, depth 0.5 mm, and \(D=1.6\times10^{-11}\) m²/s give \(\operatorname{erf}^{-1}(r)=0.3923898\) and duration approximately 7.04729 hours. The use of mass fraction assumes the constant-density scalar approximation; it is not a conversion to molar concentration. The program substitutes the computed duration back into the profile and recovers the target. No temperature recommendation follows from this arithmetic alone.

The approximation \(\operatorname{erf}z\approx z\) is not the correct leading Taylor approximation: the leading coefficient is \(2/\sqrt\pi\). More precisely, \(\operatorname{erf}z=(2/\sqrt\pi)(z-z^3/3+\cdots)\). Even if a restricted table suggests numerical proximity to the identity, inverse calculations square the inferred argument and can magnify a shape error. The code gives \(\operatorname{erf}(0.5)=0.5204999\) and \(\operatorname{erf}(0.8)=0.7421010\), which show that an uncontrolled identity substitution changes direction and size of the discrepancy across the interval.

A second tutorial uses couple end members 50% and 40%, target 42.5% at 20 mm after 40 hours. Equation (3.7) requires \(\operatorname{erf}z=0.5\). Its exact inverse yields \(D=3.0529296\times10^{-9}\) m²/s. Replacing the inverse argument by 0.5 gives \(2.7777778\times10^{-9}\) m²/s instead. The repeated-depth statement remains fourfold time in either calculation, but the inferred coefficient changes. Agreement with a scaling relation does not validate an approximate parameter estimate.

3.8 Exercises with worked solutions

1. A surface width needs a definition

Normalize the reflecting half-space thin-source solution. Derive its mean depth, second moment about the surface, and centered variance. Explain why using the whole-line Gaussian variance for every quoted surface “width” is ambiguous.

Worked solution

The half-line normalized density is \(p(x)=e^{-x^2/(4Dt)}/\sqrt{\pi Dt}\). Set \(u=x/(2\sqrt{Dt})\). Its integral is \((2/\sqrt\pi)\int_0^\infty e^{-u^2}du=1\). The first moment is \((4\sqrt{Dt}/\sqrt\pi)\int_0^\infty u e^{-u^2}du=2\sqrt{Dt/\pi}\). The second moment is \((8Dt/\sqrt\pi)\int_0^\infty u^2e^{-u^2}du=2Dt\). Subtracting the square of the mean gives \(2Dt(1-2/\pi)\). The whole-line symmetric Gaussian has zero mean and variance \(2Dt\); a half-line centered variance is smaller. The program independently integrates the moments.

2. Check a maintained-source amount balance

Starting from (3.5), derive the surface flux and integrated excess amount. Why does the infinite initial flux not imply an infinite dose at every positive time?

Worked solution

The chain rule gives \(\partial_xc=-(c_s-c_0)e^{-x^2/(4Dt)}/\sqrt{\pi Dt}\). Thus the inward surface flux is \((c_s-c_0)\sqrt{D/(\pi t)}\). Integrating this rate from zero to \(t\) gives \(2(c_s-c_0)\sqrt{Dt/\pi}\). Integrating the profile in space gives the same answer because \(\int_0^\infty\operatorname{erfc}u\,du=1/\sqrt\pi\). The integral of \(t^{-1/2}\) at zero is finite. The ideal flux diverges instantaneously but supplies a finite accumulated dose over each finite interval.

3. A finite initial layer approaches a plane source

On the infinite line let \(c(x,0)=C_0\) for \(|x|<a\) and zero elsewhere, with \(a,C_0>0\). Use Gaussian superposition to derive the profile. Identify its areal amount and its thin-layer approximation.

Worked solution

Convolution gives \(c=C_0\int_{-a}^{a}G(x-y,t)dy\). The substitution used for the couple yields \(c=(C_0/2)\{\operatorname{erf}[(x+a)/(2\sqrt{Dt})]-\operatorname{erf}[(x-a)/(2\sqrt{Dt})]\}\). Integrating in \(x\) first gives amount \(2aC_0\) because every normalized kernel integrates to one. If \(a\ll\sqrt{Dt}\) on the observation scale, the kernel varies little across the initial layer, so the convolution is approximately \(2aC_0G(x,t)\). This is (3.2) with \(M=2aC_0\). The approximation is not justified at arbitrarily short times for a fixed finite layer. The laboratory checks the finite-layer amount by quadrature.

4. Infer time and interpret a limiting threshold

Derive the treatment-time formula (3.9), reproduce the 0.5 mm tutorial result with the exact inverse function, and explain the limits as the target approaches the maintained surface value or the initial value.

Worked solution

The deficit ratio is \(r=(1.2-0.8)/(1.2-0.25)=0.4/0.95\). Its inverse error-function value is \(z=0.3923897758\). Using meters and seconds gives \(t=(0.5\times10^{-3})^2/[4(1.6\times10^{-11})z^2]\), approximately 7.04729 hours. Substitution into (3.5) recovers 0.8 in the common concentration convention. As the target approaches \(c_s\), \(r\to0\) and \(z\to0\), so a fixed positive depth reaches that exact maintained value only asymptotically. As the target approaches the initial value, \(r\to1\) and the required time tends to zero. These limits assume the initial and surface values remain distinct.

5. A logarithm convention changes the inferred coefficient

A depth-profile fit at known time gives slope \(m_{10}\) when plotting \(\log_{10}\) activity against \(x^2\). Derive the diffusivity formula and quantify the multiplicative error if that slope is treated as a natural-log slope. Which calibration factors cancel?

Worked solution

Since \(\log_{10}c=\ln c/\ln10\), the slope is \(m_{10}=-1/(4Dt\ln10)\). Therefore \(D=-1/(4tm_{10}\ln10)\). Treating it as a natural-log slope instead gives \(D_{\rm wrong}=D\ln10\). A uniform multiplicative activity-to-concentration factor only adds an intercept, as does the unknown finite dose. A depth-dependent sensitivity or additive background does not generally cancel; either can change the shape and fitted slope.

6. Choose between a fixed dose, concentration, and flux

A surface experiment reports total excess amount proportional to time while its surface concentration grows approximately as the square root of time. Which of the three half-space source models is compatible with both observations? Show the balance and identify one additional check before inferring a mechanism.

Worked solution

A finite instantaneous dose preserves its total amount and has a surface concentration decreasing as \(t^{-1/2}\), so it fails both trends. A maintained concentration fixes the surface value and gives uptake proportional to \(\sqrt t\), so it also fails. A fixed inward flux gives \(M_{\rm excess}=qt\) and \(c(0,t)-c_0=2q\sqrt{t/(\pi D)}\), matching both scalings. These observations are compatible with the boundary model, not a unique atomic mechanism. Check the entire scaled profile against (3.8), account for instrumental broadening and surface reactions, or independently measure the imposed entry rate. Several microscopic mechanisms can share the same scalar diffusion coefficient.
