A tracer penetrates much farther along a grain boundary than through the adjoining crystal. Serial sectioning, however, collects both regions together. The resulting deep tail contains information about a fast path, its storage capacity, and its exchange with the grains. Extracting a boundary diffusivity requires deciding which of those quantities the experiment can actually separate.

20.1 A boundary transports and stores solute L42 00:22

A grain is a crystal region with a particular orientation. A grain boundary is the transition between neighboring grains. Its atomic arrangement depends on their misorientation, the boundary plane, and the atomic translations and chemical state at the interface. A low-angle boundary may be interpreted as an array of dislocations; a coherent twin has a specially ordered relation between the two crystals. Calling both objects “interfaces” does not give them equal transport coefficients.

Let the tracer be a dilute distinguishable isotope or solute that does not appreciably change the host structure. Its molar concentration is \(c\), in mol m⁻³. In a fixed lattice frame, its molar flux is \(\mathbf J\), in mol m⁻² s⁻¹. We initially assume isothermal, isotropic Fickian transport inside each region: \(\mathbf J=-D\nabla c\) in the grains and \(\mathbf J_b=-D_b\nabla c_b\) inside a boundary of width \(\delta\). Both diffusivities have units m² s⁻¹. The boundary width is an effective model thickness; it need not equal a uniquely measurable geometric thickness.

Atomic disorder can change defect formation energies, jump barriers, and correlated motion. Boundaries therefore often provide rapid transport, with consequences for sintering, creep, precipitation, and film stability. This is an empirical tendency, not an ordering theorem for every boundary and species. A large diffusivity at one temperature also does not prove a smaller activation enthalpy: the prefactors may differ. At the atomic scale, vacancy, interstitial, and collective motion can all contribute. One measured macroscopic coefficient does not select one microscopic mechanism.

For a foreign solute, local chemical equilibrium may enrich the boundary. Define the dimensionless segregation factor \(s_b=c_b/c_s\), where \(c_s\) is the grain concentration immediately outside the boundary. We assume a dilute linear partition relation at fixed temperature, so \(c_b=s_b c_s\) with constant \(s_b>0\). This relation can hold while both concentrations change with time. It asserts rapid interfacial equilibration, not global equilibrium or zero flux.

For boundaries occupying volume fraction \(g\), the locally equilibrated mean concentration is \(\bar c=[1-g+g s_b]c_s\). The factor in brackets is storage capacity relative to the grain. Thus increased boundary concentration can raise the stored amount without proportionally raising the distance an individual atom travels. Transport and storage must enter the same balance.

20.2 Deriving a boundary-plus-lattice balance L42 10:30

The Fisher construction idealizes a straight boundary perpendicular to the specimen surface. Let \(z\geq0\) be depth and \(y\) the transverse coordinate, both in meters. The boundary occupies \(|y|<\delta/2\); the adjacent grains extend away on either side. There is no variation in the third direction. Away from the boundary, conservation and the scalar constitutive law give

\[\partial_t c=D(\partial_y^2c+\partial_z^2c). \tag{20.1}\]

Suppose the boundary is sufficiently thin that its concentration is nearly uniform across its width. Its amount per unit boundary-plane area is then \(\delta c_b=s_b\delta c_s\), in mol m⁻². Define \(j_L,j_R\) as fluxes directed out of the boundary into the left and right grains. They can have either sign. Integrate conservation over a boundary segment of depth \(dz\) and unit extent in the third direction. The net influx at the segment ends is \(-\partial_z(\delta J_{b,z})dz\); the side loss is \((j_L+j_R)dz\). Dividing by \(dz\) yields

\[s_b\delta\,\partial_t c_s=s_b\delta D_b\,\partial_z^2c_s-j_L-j_R. \tag{20.2}\]

Every term has units mol m⁻² s⁻¹. At the right interface, \(j_R=-D\partial_yc|_{\delta/2+}\). At the left interface, the outward direction is negative \(y\), so \(j_L=+D\partial_yc|_{-\delta/2-}\). In a symmetric profile enriched near the boundary, both outward fluxes are positive and reduce the boundary amount. Defining the normal first prevents an unnoticed sign reversal.

The grain solution takes the interface value \(c=c_s\); its normal flux provides the exchange term in (20.2). Consequently, the boundary does not diffuse independently when leakage is substantial. It feeds solute sideways into grains while conducting it deeper along \(z\). Conversely, a depleted boundary can draw solute inward. The two equations conserve their combined amount when external surfaces carry zero flux, because the internal exchange terms cancel exactly.

Boundary data matter as much as these equations. A maintained surface concentration prescribes values at \(z=0\) over time. An instantaneous thin tracer layer prescribes an initial areal amount, represented ideally by a delta distribution in depth. Those are different experiments. A measured section mean over transverse width \(W\) is \(\bar c(z,t)=W^{-1}[\int_{\mathrm{grains}}c\,dy+\delta c_b]\). A large local boundary concentration can contribute a small total signal because its sampling fraction is small.

20.3 Harrison regimes are scale comparisons L43 00:22

Let \(d\) denote a representative spacing between boundaries. The bulk penetration scale \(\ell_D=\sqrt{Dt}\) measures how far leakage spreads laterally during time \(t\). It does not by itself measure the deeper distance traveled along the fast boundary. Harrison's classification organizes the relation between leakage fields, boundary width, and grain spacing. The original classification is associated with Harrison's 1961 paper; here we derive the scale interpretation rather than assign universal numerical cutoffs to every microstructure.

In the A regime, leakage fields from neighboring boundaries overlap substantially. If transverse equilibration is rapid, the specimen behaves at large scales like an effective medium. For parallel boundaries aligned with the imposed depth gradient, the total flux is \(\bar J_z=-[(1-g)D+g s_bD_b]\partial_zc_s\). Combining this conductance with the storage derived in Section20.1 gives

\[D_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{(1-g)D+g s_bD_b}{1-g+g s_b}. \tag{20.3}\]

The familiar arithmetic average follows when \(s_b=1\). A common gradient, connected parallel paths, constant geometry, and local partition equilibrium were essential. The A regime does not require \(D_b\approx D\); a very fast boundary can coexist with overlapping slow leakage fields. An effective coefficient inferred from a broad profile does not separately identify the component coefficients.

In the B regime, lattice leakage is appreciable but neighboring leakage fields are largely isolated: qualitatively \(\delta\ll\ell_D\ll d\) when segregation is modest. Serial-section profiles may show a near-surface bulk contribution and a deep boundary-induced tail. Quantitative asymptotics impose additional conditions. For the dilute straight-boundary formulation used here, define

\[P=s_b\delta D_b,\qquad \alpha=\frac{s_b\delta}{2\sqrt{Dt}},\qquad \beta=\frac{P}{2D\sqrt{Dt}}. \tag{20.4}\]

The product \(P\) has units m³ s⁻¹; the other quantities are dimensionless. The quoted B-tail analysis requires small \(\alpha\) and large \(\beta\), conventionally tested against \(\alpha<0.1\) and \(\beta>10\) for this particular asymptotic treatment. These tests supplement grain separation, source geometry, and detection limits. Merely finding a straight-looking tail is insufficient.

In the C regime, exchange with the lattice is negligible over the observation time. The boundary approximately obeys one-dimensional diffusion. An instantaneous source then gives a Gaussian depth dependence, and a plot of the logarithm of boundary tracer concentration against \(z^2\) has slope \(-1/(4D_bt)\). The approximation concerns negligible leakage, not an assertion that the lattice diffusivity is exactly zero. The measured amount may be extremely small because only the boundary volume carries the signal.

20.4 A B tail identifies a product L43 16:54

For the instantaneous-source B asymptotic, let \(m_B=-d\ln(\bar c/c_{\mathrm{ref}})/d(z^{6/5})>0\), where \(c_{\mathrm{ref}}\) is any fixed concentration reference. It only makes the logarithm dimensionless and does not affect its slope. The Le Claire analysis gives the following cited inverse relation, not a result derived by assuming that a tail is Gaussian:

\[P=1.308\sqrt{D/t}\,m_B^{-5/3}. \tag{20.5}\]

The formula and its source conditions are given explicitly in Vaidya and coauthors, PDF page5, Eq.(3). The slope has units m⁻⁶/⁵; its power contributes m². The square-root factor contributes m s⁻¹, producing the required m³ s⁻¹. Using the concentration itself instead of its logarithm would fail this dimensional and normalization check. The relation is an asymptotic inference tool; the present chapter does not reproduce the exact integral solution from which its numerical constant arises.

If \(s_b\delta\) increases tenfold while \(D_b\) decreases tenfold, the product is unchanged. For example, the synthetic pairs \((s_b\delta,D_b)=(5\times10^{-9}\,\mathrm m,10^{-12}\,\mathrm{m^2s^{-1}})\) and \((5\times10^{-8}\,\mathrm m,10^{-13}\,\mathrm{m^2s^{-1}})\) both give \(P=5\times10^{-21}\)m³s⁻¹. A valid B-tail product alone cannot distinguish them. Their complete profiles and validity parameters need not coincide outside the asymptotic window.

A C-regime experiment can independently determine \(D_b\), allowing \(P/D_b=s_b\delta\) if both observations describe comparable boundary states at the same temperature. In practice they often require different temperatures. Extrapolation then adds assumptions about Arrhenius behavior, structural stability, segregation, and annealing history. Assigning a conventional thickness, such as a half-nanometer, converts the measured capacity length into an inferred segregation factor; it does not turn that thickness into an independently measured fact.

For a dilute equilibrium partition law write \(s_b=s_0\exp[-\Delta H_{\mathrm{seg}}/(RT)]\), where \(R\) is the molar gas constant, \(T\) absolute temperature, and \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{seg}}<0\) means favorable segregation. With constant thickness and \(D_b=D_{b0}\exp[-Q_b/(RT)]\), the product has apparent enthalpy \(Q_P=Q_b+\Delta H_{\mathrm{seg}}\). Favorable segregation can therefore make the product's slope smaller than the boundary mobility slope. A positive binding-enthalpy convention would write the same physics with the opposite sign in the definition.

20.5 A conservative two-region calculation L42 19:40

The reproducible laboratory ch20.py solves a dimensionless version of \(c_t=\nabla\cdot(D\nabla c)\) in a closed rectangle. Transverse position runs from −1 to1 and depth from0 to4. The central strip has diffusivity20, the surrounding material1, and segregation is absent. An initial pulse occupies the first depth cell. This finite-width, finite-domain construction demonstrates exchange and conservation; it is not a numerical evaluation of the infinite-domain B-tail formula.

Each shared face transfers one amount from one cell to its neighbor, with the same amount subtracted from the donor. Harmonic face diffusivities enforce a common flux across discontinuous material coefficients. With grid spacings \(h_y,h_z\), the code chooses a time step below \([2D_{\max}(h_y^{-2}+h_z^{-2})]^{-1}\). All off-diagonal weights are nonnegative and their sum is at most one, so the update preserves nonnegative concentrations. Exterior face fluxes vanish. The outputs check combined mass and minimum concentration; they do not certify continuum convergence.

At dimensionless time0.03, the stored total amount remains2 to rounding error in both runs. The depth second moment rises from0.0602732 for uniform material to0.116339 with the fast strip. The latter occupies approximately0.04918 of the discretized width. Although the total amount is unchanged, some atoms travel farther before leaking into the adjacent region. Refinement should compare moments and resolved profiles while holding the physical strip width fixed; changing the number of strip cells without checking that width changes the model.

A separate SI-unit design calculation uses \(D=10^{-18}\)m²s⁻¹, \(D_b=10^{-12}\)m²s⁻¹, \(s_b=10\), \(\delta=5\times10^{-10}\)m, \(d=10^{-5}\)m, and \(t=3600\)s. It gives \(\ell_D=6\times10^{-8}\)m, \(\alpha=0.0416667\), \(\beta=41666.7\), and \(\ell_D/d=0.006\). These satisfy the stated B asymptotic tests and separated-field scale comparison. Specimen geometry, source quality, and detection dynamic range still require experimental checks.

20.6 Exercises and worked solutions

  1. Internal flux cancellation. Integrate (20.1) in the grains and (20.2) along a boundary with closed external faces. Prove combined conservation.

    Solution

    The divergence theorem converts each grain integral into its boundary fluxes. At a grain–boundary interface, the grain receives \(j_L\) or \(j_R\). Equation(20.2) loses precisely their sum. Along-depth endpoint fluxes and exterior grain fluxes vanish by the closed conditions. Thus \(d[\int c\,dV+\int\delta c_b\,dA]/dt=0\). Concentrations may change while this total remains fixed.

  2. Segregation capacity. Derive the effective coefficient for equilibrated parallel boundaries. Explain why using only the numerator in (20.3) is incorrect when segregation is strong.

    Solution

    Mean storage is \(Kc_s\), with \(K=1-g+gs_b\). Mean flux is \(-A\partial_zc_s\), with \(A=(1-g)D+gs_bD_b\). For constant coefficients, conservation gives \(K\partial_tc_s=A\partial_z^2c_s\); equivalently \(\partial_t\bar c=(A/K)\partial_z^2\bar c\). The numerator alone measures conductance relative to \(c_s\), whereas the measured mean includes boundary storage.

  3. Product ambiguity. Can a perfect B-tail measurement distinguish the two synthetic parameter pairs in Section20.4?

    Solution

    Not through the product inversion: both give identical \(P\). An independently known \(D\) removes one nuisance input but does not split \(s_b\delta\) from \(D_b\). A C measurement, a direct segregation measurement, or a richer validated full-profile model supplies additional information. Each proposed measurement must still be sensitive to the parameter it is intended to determine.

  4. C-tail inversion. Express \(D_b\) in terms of the negative slope \(m_C\) of \(\ln(\bar c/c_{\mathrm{ref}})\) versus \(z^2\). State two failure conditions.

    Solution

    The Gaussian exponent is \(-z^2/(4D_bt)\), hence \(D_b=-1/(4tm_C)\). The slope has units m⁻², yielding m²s⁻¹. Leakage changes the model, and a finite initial source width broadens the profile independently of diffusion. Depth-resolution broadening is another possible bias. A straight fitted line over a short range cannot by itself exclude these alternatives.

  5. Temperature slope. Under the assumptions of Section20.4, why can \(Q_P<Q_b\)?

    Solution

    Multiply the Arrhenius forms to get \(P=s_0\delta D_{b0}\exp[-(Q_b+\Delta H_{\mathrm{seg}})/(RT)]\). Favorable segregation has negative \(\Delta H_{\mathrm{seg}}\), so \(Q_P\) is reduced. This result assumes no temperature-dependent thickness or additional prefactor variation. It does not imply that the jump barrier itself fell.

  6. Design judgment. A long anneal produces a nearly Gaussian section profile. Is it legitimate to report the fitted coefficient as a boundary diffusivity?

    Solution

    No such identification follows from shape alone. An A-regime effective medium can be Gaussian, as can a leakage-free C-regime boundary. Compute the lattice penetration length from independent lattice data, compare it with grain spacing and boundary width, and inspect the tracer's sampled amount. Varying time or grain size tests whether overlap drives the profile. Report an effective coefficient until the pathway assignment is supported.

The strongest inference is often a deliberately limited one: a measured product or an effective coefficient, accompanied by the assumptions under which it was extracted. That limited result can guide the next experiment more reliably than an unjustified microscopic label.
