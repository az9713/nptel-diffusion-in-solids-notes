A protective coating can fail even when its surface remains intact. Atoms can migrate through the coating, react at an internal interface, and change the substrate. Predicting service life requires an amount balance, a transport law, and boundary conditions. A measured diffusion coefficient supplies only one part of that calculation.

Source spine: official lectures 1–3 and the conservation/calculus parts of Tutorial 1 (lecture 5). The transform problems from that tutorial belong to Chapter 2. Examples, derivations, numerical inputs, and diagrams here are original teaching constructions unless identified otherwise. The synthetic coating model is not a measured engineering material.

1.1 Materials processes and the conserved amount L1 12:39

Diffusion is transport associated with microscopic motion and its statistical imbalance across space. Individual atoms can move in both directions while a nonzero net amount crosses an imaginary surface. Even when the net transport vanishes, microscopic motion can continue. A concentration profile describes the spatial distribution of an ensemble; it does not trace an individual atom's trajectory.

In a solid, atoms occupy environments constrained by the local structure. An atom may move between interstitial sites, exchange with a vacancy, or travel along a boundary. A vacancy is an unoccupied atomic site; an interstitial atom occupies a position between the regular host sites. Those mechanisms determine rates, but conservation can be established before selecting one. Chapters 5–9 will connect the continuum description to atomic structure and jump statistics.

Several materials processes require such transport. Carburization introduces carbon into a material from its surroundings. In interdiffusion, components of contacting materials redistribute and may form new phases. A phase is a region with a specified thermodynamic state and structure; adjacent phases can have different compositions. Oxide growth can require oxygen or metal transport through an existing oxide. These processes have different boundary conditions and driving forces, even though each must account for transported matter.

The presence of atomic motion does not establish that diffusion is the rate-limiting step. For example, a growing phase can be limited by delivery through an existing layer, by an interface reaction, or by a coupled sequence. A demonstration of ink spreading in water can also involve convection, the collective motion of the fluid. It is a qualitative motivation for dispersal, not an isolated measurement of molecular diffusivity. We will use an idealized solid coating so that collective fluid flow does not enter the first model.

Let a planar coating occupy \(0\leq x\leq L\), where position \(x\) and thickness \(L>0\) are measured in meters. Its cross-sectional area is \(A\), in square meters, and elapsed time is \(t\), in seconds. Let \(c(x,t)\) be the amount concentration of one named species, in mol/m³. The amount within a slice from \(a\) to \(b\) is

\[ n_{[a,b]}(t)=A\int_a^b c(x,t)\,dx. \tag{1.1} \]

The slice boundaries are fixed in the selected reference frame. This matters: if a boundary moves, matter can cross it because the boundary itself sweeps through the material. The first model uses a stationary solid, fixed geometry, a uniform temperature, and no reaction producing or removing the tracked species inside the coating. Reactions at its outer faces may still set boundary fluxes.

Concentration must name what is counted. A particle-number concentration has units of particles/m³; a mole fraction is dimensionless and counts one component relative to all components. They are interchangeable only after specifying the total site or molar concentration. We use molar concentration here. Consequently, every flux and source term below counts moles of the same species.

1.2 Derivatives, gradients, and local flux L2 03:23 L3 01:22

A concentration difference alone does not specify a local driving gradient. For a fixed face-to-face concentration difference, a thinner coating has a larger magnitude of the average gradient; its local gradient still depends on the profile. At fixed time, the spatial derivative is the limit \(\partial_x c=\lim_{h\to0}[c(x+h,t)-c(x,t)]/h\), when this limit exists. Its units are mol/m⁴. The partial derivative holds the other variable fixed; \(\partial_t c\) instead measures concentration change at a fixed position.

Differentiability is a local assumption about the continuum field. A sharp interface between phases may have a concentration jump, so one cannot insert a derivative across that jump into a smooth-field formula. The flux laws apply inside each phase; interface balances connect them. Even for a smooth graph, a horizontal tangent need not be a maximum or minimum. The function \(x^3\) has zero derivative at the origin and keeps increasing. Extrema require an appropriate sign change or additional derivative conditions.

A useful arithmetic check concerns the dimensionless function \(y=2x^2\). Its derivative is \(4x\); at \(x=1\) the point is \((1,2)\) and the slope is 4. Therefore its tangent is \(y-2=4(x-1)\), or \(y=4x-2\). This explicit calculation corrects inconsistent numbers in the caption discussion near L2 11:36. Tangent construction later supplies chemical potentials from free-energy curves, so the geometric operation deserves a check.

Definition 1.1 — molar flux. Let \(J(x,t)\) be the signed amount crossing a unit area normal to the positive \(x\) direction per unit time, in mol/(m²·s). A positive value means net transport toward increasing \(x\). For a uniform flux over area \(A\), the amount transfer rate is \(AJ\), in mol/s.

The simplest constitutive assumption is Fick's first law:

\[ J(x,t)=-D\,\frac{\partial c}{\partial x}(x,t),\qquad D>0. \tag{1.2} \]

The scalar \(D\), the diffusivity or diffusion coefficient of this model, has units m²/s. Multiplying it by the concentration gradient gives the required mol/(m²·s). If the concentration decreases toward positive \(x\), the derivative is negative and the flux is positive. The negative sign expresses transport down concentration for this particular positive scalar closure. Chapter 10 will replace the concentration gradient with a thermodynamic driving force when that closure is inadequate.

Equation (1.2) does not require a steady profile. It relates flux to the gradient at the stated position and time. During transient diffusion, evaluate the gradient and flux at the same position and time; either may vary with time. A spatially or composition-dependent \(D\) can still be used locally, provided the material model justifies it. Chemical reactions do not invalidate the definition of flux; they introduce source terms into the amount balance. External fields and coupled species generally require additional constitutive terms.

In three dimensions, let \(\mathbf{x}=(x,y,z)\) be position and \(\mathbf{e}_x,\mathbf{e}_y,\mathbf{e}_z\) the Cartesian unit vectors. The gradient is \(\nabla c=\mathbf{e}_x\partial_x c+\mathbf{e}_y\partial_y c+\mathbf{e}_z\partial_z c\). For isotropic diffusion, meaning the same scalar response in every direction, \(\mathbf{J}=-D\nabla c\). Components must remain attached to their unit vectors; adding three scalar derivatives does not by itself produce a vector.

Fick's original 1855 paper, On liquid diffusion, establishes the historical attribution. The continuum formulation and examples developed here are independent derivations. A historical successful constitutive relation does not establish its validity for every solid, driving field, or microscopic time scale.

1.3 From an amount balance to Fick's second law L3 06:28

Take an arbitrary fixed slice \([a,b]\) of the coating. Its left face receives \(AJ(a,t)\) moles per second, while its right face loses \(AJ(b,t)\). If \(S(x,t)\) is the rate of local production per volume, in mol/(m³·s), inflow minus outflow plus production gives

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\left[A\int_a^b c\,dx\right] =A[J(a,t)-J(b,t)]+A\int_a^b S\,dx. \tag{1.3} \]

Both sides have units mol/s. A missing slice volume in a local accumulation term would make the units disagree. This is the deliberate diagnostic in L3 near 10:38: a concentration rate must be multiplied by volume before it can be compared with total transfer rates. Dividing by area and using the fundamental theorem of calculus transforms (1.3) into \(\int_a^b(\partial_t c+\partial_x J-S)dx=0\). For smooth fields, an integral vanishing over every slice implies that its continuous integrand vanishes:

Derived balance 1.1. \[ \partial_t c=-\partial_x J+S. \tag{1.4} \] This local conservation statement does not yet select a diffusion mechanism.

For a nonreacting species set \(S=0\). Substitution of the constitutive relation (1.2) then gives the diffusion equation:

\[ \partial_t c=\partial_x\!\left(D\partial_x c\right). \quad\text{If }D\text{ is constant:}\quad \partial_t c=D\partial_{xx}c. \tag{1.5} \]

The last simplification requires constancy in space, including any indirect spatial dependence through composition or temperature. If \(D=D(c)\) is differentiable, the right side is \(D(c)\partial_{xx}c+D'(c)(\partial_x c)^2\). Dropping the second term changes the model. A prescribed diffusivity depending on position gives a linear variable-coefficient equation; dependence on the unknown concentration generally makes the equation nonlinear.

The sign has a direct physical check. At a smooth local concentration maximum, a constant-\(D\) profile has \(\partial_{xx}c\leq0\), so its concentration cannot increase through source-free diffusion alone. At a minimum the inequality reverses. Flattening peaks and filling troughs are consequences of the local curvature, rather than a rule that concentration must decrease at every position. Solute entering from a boundary can raise concentrations throughout part of the sample.

Integrating (1.4) over the full coating gives \(d n_{[0,L]}/dt=A[J(0,t)-J(L,t)]\) when \(S=0\). Impermeable faces have zero normal flux and therefore conserve total amount. Holding a nonzero surface concentration instead permits exchange with an external reservoir. In that case the amount inside the specimen need not remain constant, even though the specimen-plus-reservoir system conserves the species.

In three dimensions, the corresponding equation is \(\partial_t c=-\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}+S\). Here \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{J}=\partial_xJ_x+\partial_yJ_y+\partial_zJ_z\) is the divergence, the net outward flux per volume in an infinitesimal limit. With isotropic constant diffusivity and no sources, \(\partial_t c=D\nabla^2c\), where \(\nabla^2c=\partial_{xx}c+\partial_{yy}c+\partial_{zz}c\). Geometry will change the coordinate expression of this operator, not the conservation principle.

The executable laboratory also checks a discrete version of conservation without assuming a particular flux law. For adjacent cells, the outgoing face flux of one cell is the incoming face flux of the next. Summing the cell balances cancels all interior faces. Only the two external face fluxes remain. This cancellation, called telescoping, is the reason a conservative numerical method can preserve global amount even before its local transport approximation has converged.

Figure 1.1 shows a profile that changes while preserving its amount. To verify the illustration, define dimensionless position \(\xi=x/L\), dimensionless time \(\tau=Dt/L^2\), and normalized concentration \(u=c/c_{\rm ref}\), where \(c_{\rm ref}>0\) is a reference concentration. The function \(u=1+\tfrac12\cos(\pi\xi)e^{-\pi^2\tau}\) satisfies \(\partial_\tau u=\partial_{\xi\xi}u\) by differentiation. Its derivative in \(\xi\) vanishes at both faces, and integrating its cosine term from zero to one gives zero. Its mean therefore remains one while its peak decays.

1.4 Steady transport and resistance L3 04:24

A steady state has \(\partial_t c=0\) in the chosen frame. With no internal production, equation (1.4) then requires \(\partial_x J=0\): the flux is constant across a planar coating of uniform area. Steadiness does not require zero flux. Two reservoirs can sustain a concentration difference and a continuous flow through an unchanging profile.

Let the two prescribed face concentrations be \(c_L=c(0)\) and \(c_R=c(L)\). For a uniform constant diffusivity, integrate \(dc/dx=-J/D\) from one face to the other. This yields

\[ c(x)=c_L+\frac{c_R-c_L}{L}x, \qquad J=\frac{D}{L}(c_L-c_R). \tag{1.6} \]

For a prescribed gradient, doubling \(D\) doubles the flux. For prescribed face concentrations, doubling thickness halves the flux. These are different experimental constraints. A claim about dependence on distance must state which quantity is held fixed. A linear profile is a result of this steady, source-free, planar, constant-diffusivity model; it is not the definition of steady diffusion.

A layered coating illustrates why the constraints matter. Let layer \(i\) have thickness \(L_i\) and diffusivity \(D_i\). Assume no interfacial storage or reaction and the same concentration convention in both layers. Further assume concentration continuity at their interface: there is no equilibrium partition jump in this example. Each layer carries the same steady flux and has concentration drop \(JL_i/D_i\). Adding the drops gives

\[ J=\frac{c_L-c_R}{\sum_i L_i/D_i}. \tag{1.7} \]

The quantity \(L_i/D_i\) is a diffusion resistance per area for this concentration convention, with units s/m. A thick slow layer can dominate the sum. An arithmetic average of diffusivities would incorrectly treat successive layers as if they were parallel pathways experiencing the same gradient. If partitioning occurs at the interface, continuity of flux still holds under the stated no-storage assumptions, but continuity of concentration does not; the relation must then include the partition law.

For synthetic values \(L_1=10^{-3}\) m, \(D_1=10^{-12}\) m²/s, \(L_2=2\times10^{-3}\) m, and \(D_2=4\times10^{-12}\) m²/s, the two resistances are \(10^9\) and \(5\times10^8\) s/m. Taking \(c_L=2\) and \(c_R=0.5\) mol/m³ gives \(J=10^{-9}\) mol/(m²·s). The interface concentration is \(2-J(10^9)=1\) mol/m³. The second layer independently gives \((1-0.5)/(5\times10^8)=10^{-9}\), verifying continuity.

The laboratory's inputs are deliberately synthetic. Its assertions verify the resistance calculation and interface balance, not the suitability of those constants for a real coating. A measured service-life prediction would also require temperature dependence, evolving phases, reservoir behavior, and a failure criterion. The next chapter addresses the initial and boundary data needed before a transient diffusion problem has a unique physical meaning.

1.5 Cylinders, spheres, and geometric conservation L3 15:45

A coating on a wire or spherical particle does not have the same transport area at every radius. Let \(r>0\) be distance from the cylinder axis or sphere center. Assume radial symmetry: concentration is independent of angle and, for the cylinder, axial position. The radial component of flux is \(J_r=-D\partial_r c\), positive outward. Let \(A(r)\) be the area of the surface through which this flux passes.

A thin radial shell of thickness \(\Delta r\) contains approximately \(A(r)c(r,t)\Delta r\) moles. Inflow minus outflow is \(A(r)J_r(r,t)-A(r+\Delta r)J_r(r+\Delta r,t)\). Dividing by shell volume and taking the limit yields \(\partial_t c=-A(r)^{-1}\partial_r[A(r)J_r]\). For a cylinder of fixed length, area is proportional to \(r\); for a sphere it is proportional to \(r^2\). Therefore

\[ \partial_t c=\frac{1}{r^m}\partial_r\!\left(r^mD\partial_r c\right), \qquad m=0,1,2\text{ for planar, cylindrical, spherical symmetry}. \tag{1.8} \]

This derivation exposes the geometric factor rather than treating it as a coordinate-table identity. With constant diffusivity, cylindrical diffusion contains \(D(\partial_{rr}c+r^{-1}\partial_rc)\); spherical diffusion contains \(D(\partial_{rr}c+2r^{-1}\partial_rc)\). At the center of a smooth symmetric profile, \(\partial_rc(0,t)=0\). The apparent coordinate singularity must be treated by its smooth limit, not by dividing a numerical zero by zero.

More generally, cylindrical coordinates \((r,\theta,z)\) use azimuthal angle \(\theta\) and axial position \(z\). The gradient is \(\nabla c=\mathbf e_r\partial_rc+\mathbf e_\theta r^{-1}\partial_\theta c+\mathbf e_z\partial_zc\). The factor \(1/r\) converts angular change into change per arc length. Constant-\(D\) diffusion is then \(\partial_t c=D[r^{-1}\partial_r(r\partial_rc)+r^{-2}\partial_{\theta\theta}c+\partial_{zz}c]\). Radial symmetry removes the angular and axial terms, recovering (1.8).

Steady radial transport illustrates a useful distinction. The total rate \(\dot n=A(r)J_r\) is independent of radius when there is no source. The flux density \(J_r\) itself is not constant because the transport area changes. For a hollow sphere with inner and outer radii \(a<b\), and corresponding maintained concentrations \(c_a,c_b\), integration gives

\[ \dot n=\frac{4\pi D(c_a-c_b)}{1/a-1/b},\qquad c(r)=c_a-\frac{\dot n}{4\pi D}\left(\frac1a-\frac1r\right). \tag{1.9} \]

The positive denominator gives outward transport for \(c_a>c_b\). To check the thin-shell limit, write \(b=a+L\). Then \((1/a-1/b)^{-1}=ab/L\), so the rate tends to \(4\pi a^2D(c_a-c_b)/L\) when \(L/a\to0\). This is the planar formula multiplied by the nearly constant spherical area.

For a hollow cylinder of length \(H\), the same integration instead gives \(\dot n=2\pi HD(c_a-c_b)/\ln(b/a)\). The logarithm arises from integrating \(1/r\). A linear radial profile would incorrectly impose a constant flux density rather than a constant total transfer rate. Geometry changes the profile even when the underlying material diffusivity is unchanged.

1.6 Directional diffusion and the tensor model L3 25:02

A crystal can present different jump environments in different directions. A scalar coefficient cannot describe that directional response. At fixed temperature and within a linear reciprocal diffusion model, replace it by a symmetric positive-definite matrix \(\mathbf D\), the diffusion tensor. Its Cartesian components have units m²/s, and

\[ J_i=-\sum_{j=1}^3D_{ij}\partial_jc,\qquad \mathbf J=-\mathbf D\nabla c. \tag{1.10} \]

Positive definiteness means \(\mathbf u^\mathsf T\mathbf D\mathbf u>0\) for every nonzero real vector \(\mathbf u\). It ensures \(\mathbf J\cdot\nabla c=-\nabla c^\mathsf T\mathbf D\nabla c\leq0\) in this concentration-gradient model. The flux can nevertheless have a component perpendicular to the gradient. Down-gradient projection and exact antiparallel alignment are different assertions.

The spectral theorem for real symmetric matrices supplies orthogonal principal directions, in which \(\mathbf D\) is diagonal with positive entries \(D_1,D_2,D_3\). For a unit direction vector \(\mathbf n\), impose a gradient \(g\mathbf n\), where the scalar \(g\) has concentration-gradient units. The flux component along that direction is \(\mathbf n\cdot\mathbf J=-g\mathbf n^\mathsf T\mathbf D\mathbf n\). Thus the projected directional diffusivity is

\[ D_{\mathbf n}=\mathbf n^\mathsf T\mathbf D\mathbf n =D_1n_1^2+D_2n_2^2+D_3n_3^2. \tag{1.11} \]

The components \(n_i\) in (1.11) are measured along the principal axes and obey \(\sum_i n_i^2=1\). Therefore the projected coefficient lies between the smallest and largest principal values. If all three equal \(D_0\), every direction has coefficient \(D_0\). If two equal \(D_\perp\) and the third is \(D_\parallel\), a direction at angle \(\vartheta\) to the third principal axis has \(D_{\mathbf n}=D_\perp\sin^2\vartheta+D_\parallel\cos^2\vartheta\).

For a synthetic two-dimensional example, take \(\mathbf D=\operatorname{diag}(1,4)\times10^{-12}\) m²/s and \(\mathbf n=(1,1)/\sqrt2\). A gradient \(1000\mathbf n\) mol/m⁴ produces flux components approximately \((-0.7071,-2.8284)\times10^{-9}\) mol/(m²·s). The projected coefficient is \(2.5\times10^{-12}\) m²/s. Projection on the perpendicular direction \((-1,1)/\sqrt2\) gives \(-1.5\times10^{-9}\) mol/(m²·s), confirming a transverse component. The code computes these values directly.

Three eigenvalues describe the principal strengths of a symmetric tensor. They do not determine its components in an arbitrary laboratory frame without the principal-axis orientation. With an orthogonal matrix \(\mathbf Q\) whose columns are principal directions, the laboratory tensor is \(\mathbf Q\operatorname{diag}(D_1,D_2,D_3)\mathbf Q^\mathsf T\). Crystal symmetry can reduce the number of independent parameters, but one must state which symmetry and which frame are being used. Boundary constraints can also prevent the free transverse flux assumed in (1.11), so an apparent coefficient in a constrained specimen needs its own boundary-value calculation.

The coating model now has a complete local structure: an amount balance and a selected flux law. It still needs an initial concentration and enough boundary information to determine a trajectory. The same diffusivity can produce a Gaussian plume, a maintained-surface penetration profile, or decay of a finite specimen's modes. Those differences are the subject of Chapters 2–4.

1.7 Exercises with worked solutions

1. Amount, concentration, and missing volume

A source-free fixed slice has area \(A\), thickness \(\Delta x\), and incoming and outgoing fluxes \(J_-\) and \(J_+\). Derive its average concentration rate and state which quantities must be fixed. Explain why equating \(A(J_--J_+)\) directly with a concentration rate is dimensionally wrong.

Worked solution

The slice amount is \(A\Delta x\bar c\), where \(\bar c\) is its volume-averaged concentration. Fixed area and thickness give \(A\Delta x\,d\bar c/dt=A(J_--J_+)\). Thus \(d\bar c/dt=(J_--J_+)/\Delta x\). The total transfer rate has units mol/s, while the concentration rate has units mol/(m³·s). Volume is required to connect them. A moving or swelling slice additionally needs the derivative of its volume and the correct relative flux through its faces.

2. A steady profile need not be linear

Let a planar source-free layer have prescribed spatial diffusivity \(D(x)=D_0(1+\alpha x/L)\), with \(D_0>0\) and dimensionless \(\alpha>-1\). Derive its steady flux from the two face concentrations. Recover (1.6) as \(\alpha\to0\).

Worked solution

Conservation makes \(J\) constant. Integrating \(dc=-Jdx/D(x)\) gives \(c_L-c_R=J L\ln(1+\alpha)/(D_0\alpha)\) for nonzero \(\alpha\). Hence \(J=(D_0/L)(c_L-c_R)\alpha/\ln(1+\alpha)\). The ratio remains positive because numerator and logarithm have the same sign. Since \(\ln(1+\alpha)/\alpha\to1\), the uniform result follows. The profile is logarithmic in \(1+\alpha x/L\), so steady state alone does not imply a line.

3. A local steady condition is not a global solution

In dimensionless coordinates consider the tutorial field \(\mathbf J=(3x^2,2my,z^2)\), with constant parameter \(m\). Find the value making accumulation zero at \((1,1,1)\). Does that value make the field source-free steady everywhere?

Worked solution

The divergence is \(6x+2m+2z\). At the specified point, zero divergence requires \(6+2m+2=0\), so \(m=-4\). Elsewhere the divergence is \(6x+2z-8\), which vanishes only on a plane. A local condition cannot establish a globally steady field. This example adopts dimensionless coordinates because otherwise the polynomial coefficients would need distinct units; a bare polynomial flux is not an SI material law.

4. Compare a spherical shell with a planar estimate

For the hollow sphere in (1.9), express the exact rate divided by the planar estimate based on the inner area \(4\pi a^2\) and thickness \(L=b-a\). Explain the sign of the error.

Worked solution

The exact rate is \(4\pi Dab(c_a-c_b)/(b-a)\). The inner-area planar estimate is \(4\pi Da^2(c_a-c_b)/(b-a)\). Their ratio is \(b/a=1+L/a\). The exact magnitude is larger because most shells offer more area than the inner face. As the relative thickness tends to zero, the ratio tends to one. Equal endpoint concentrations make both rates zero, so the ratio should be interpreted through their conductances rather than division by a zero rate.

5. Tensor bounds and directional alignment

Prove that the projected diffusivity lies between the smallest and largest eigenvalues of a symmetric positive-definite tensor. Under what condition is the flux exactly opposite a nonzero gradient? Verify the transverse flux in the synthetic example.

Worked solution

In principal coordinates \(D_{\mathbf n}=\sum_iD_i n_i^2\). The nonnegative weights sum to one, so this is a weighted mean of the eigenvalues and obeys their minimum/maximum bounds. Exact antiparallel alignment requires \(\mathbf D\mathbf n=\lambda\mathbf n\), meaning the gradient direction belongs to an eigenspace. In the example, the perpendicular unit vector is \((-1,1)/\sqrt2\). Dotting it into the computed flux gives \(10^{-9}(1/2-4/2)=-1.5\times10^{-9}\) mol/(m²·s). A scalar projected coefficient does not encode this transverse component.

6. Diagnose a numerical conservation error

A finite-volume program stores one flux per cell face but computes an interior face separately for each neighboring cell. Explain how inconsistent values can create matter. Design a check that detects this without requiring an exact continuum solution, and apply it to the laboratory's face fluxes.

Worked solution

Each interior transfer must leave one cell and enter the adjacent one with equal magnitude. If the two evaluations disagree, their sum leaves a residual source. Store a single shared face flux, or explicitly assert equality before updating. Then compare the sum of all cell amount changes with the time-integrated external inflow minus outflow. In the laboratory, the face values are \((0.2,-0.1,0.4,0.05,0)\) and the four cell widths are 0.25 in a consistent synthetic unit system. Summing \(-[J_{j+1/2}-J_{j-1/2}]\) gives \(0.2-0=0.2\). The code returns this value to floating-point precision. This test checks conservation; it does not check that the flux approximation has the right continuum limit.

"""Synthetic checks for conservation, layered resistance, and anisotropy.

Run from any directory. Requires NumPy. Writes no files and uses no network.
All quantities are SI. Inputs are pedagogical, not measured material data.
"""

import json
import numpy as np


def run():
    # Series layers share a constant flux; concentration drops add.
    thickness = np.array([1e-3, 2e-3])
    diffusivity = np.array([1e-12, 4e-12])
    concentrations = (2.0, 0.5)
    resistance = thickness / diffusivity
    flux = (concentrations[0] - concentrations[1]) / resistance.sum()
    interface = concentrations[0] - flux * resistance[0]
    assert np.isclose(interface, 1.0)
    assert np.allclose(
        flux,
        (concentrations[0] - interface) / resistance[0],
        rtol=1e-12,
        atol=0,
    )
    assert np.allclose(
        flux,
        (interface - concentrations[1]) / resistance[1],
        rtol=1e-12,
        atol=0,
    )

    # A finite-volume divergence telescopes exactly, independent of closure.
    face_flux = np.array([0.2, -0.1, 0.4, 0.05, 0.0])
    dx = 0.25
    rates = -(face_flux[1:] - face_flux[:-1]) / dx
    global_rate = rates.sum() * dx
    assert np.isclose(global_rate, face_flux[0] - face_flux[-1])

    # A gradient at 45 degrees need not give a parallel diffusive flux.
    tensor = np.diag([1e-12, 4e-12])
    direction = np.array([1.0, 1.0]) / np.sqrt(2.0)
    gradient = 1000.0 * direction
    vector_flux = -tensor @ gradient
    projected_D = direction @ tensor @ direction
    assert np.isclose(projected_D / 1e-12, 2.5)
    transverse = vector_flux @ np.array([-direction[1], direction[0]])
    assert abs(transverse) > 0
    assert np.isclose(transverse, -1.5e-9, rtol=1e-12, atol=0)
    # Independently integrate the variable-coefficient steady resistance.
    x = np.linspace(0, 1, 20001)
    alpha = 0.5
    numerical_resistance = np.trapezoid(1 / (1 + alpha * x), x)
    exact_resistance = np.log1p(alpha) / alpha
    assert abs(numerical_resistance - exact_resistance) < 1e-9
    # Exact-mode mass, no-flux faces, and PDE derivative identity.
    for tau in (0, 0.1, 0.5):
        u = 1 + 0.5 * np.cos(np.pi * x) * np.exp(-(np.pi**2) * tau)
        assert abs(np.trapezoid(u, x) - 1) < 1e-12
        du_dtau = (
            -0.5 * np.pi**2 * np.cos(np.pi * x) * np.exp(-(np.pi**2) * tau)
        )
        du_dxi2 = (
            -0.5 * np.pi**2 * np.cos(np.pi * x) * np.exp(-(np.pi**2) * tau)
        )
        assert np.allclose(du_dtau, du_dxi2)
    # Check the tangent, divergence, radial resistance, and eigenvalue bounds.
    assert 4 * 1 - 2 == 2
    assert 6 * 1 + 2 * (-4) + 2 * 1 == 0
    assert 6 * 0 + 2 * (-4) + 2 * 0 != 0
    a, b = 1.0, 1.2
    assert np.isclose((1 / (1 / a - 1 / b)) / (a * a / (b - a)), b / a)
    for angle in np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 101):
        unit = np.array([np.cos(angle), np.sin(angle)])
        value = unit @ tensor @ unit
        assert 1e-12 * (1 - 1e-12) <= value <= 4e-12 * (1 + 1e-12)
    return {
        "synthetic_inputs": True,
        "layer_resistances_s_per_m": resistance.tolist(),
        "steady_flux_mol_per_m2_s": float(flux),
        "interface_concentration_mol_per_m3": float(interface),
        "global_amount_rate_per_area": float(global_rate),
        "directional_D_m2_per_s": float(projected_D),
        "vector_flux_mol_per_m2_s": vector_flux.tolist(),
        "transverse_flux_mol_per_m2_s": float(transverse),
        "tangent_to_2x2_at_1": {"slope": 4, "intercept": -2},
        "variable_D_resistance_ratio": float(exact_resistance),
        "spherical_exact_to_inner_planar_rate_ratio": float(b / a),
        "mode_mean_at_all_sampled_times": 1.0,
    }
